Cancer project management

Here is a useful and grim description of what happens after a cancer diagnosis:

"You need to be doggedly persistent on scheduling things."

JJ Maxwell was diagnosed with squamous cell carcinoma in his mouth in May. His account is not mainly about the biology of cancer. It is about calling offices repeatedly, asking about cancellations, chasing promised genetic testing, arranging second opinions, requesting another pathology review, reading the standard-of-care flow charts and keeping notes after every appointment.

"No one cares more about your case than you, so take control and send lots of follow-ups."

This is obviously true in one sense and horrifying in another. Cancer treatment is conducted by highly trained specialists using sophisticated imaging, pathology, genetics and surgery. But the system integrating those specialists is apparently you, a sick person with a phone and a new folder on your Mac.

Maxwell found that going to a major cancer center improved not just expertise but speed. At MD Anderson, a doctor called a pathologist he knew and got a question answered within 20 minutes. Elsewhere, Maxwell writes, the answer might have taken days or never arrived. One of the medical advantages of a top cancer center is that its internal social graph has lower latency.

There is also an interesting AI point. Maxwell says that models initially pushed him toward "you don't have cancer," which is not what you want from your probabilistic reassurance machine. But after diagnosis, he used AI to summarize dictated appointment notes, generate follow-up questions and help him work through treatment guidelines.

That division of labor seems sensible. Do not let the chatbot tell you that the alarming thing is probably nothing. Do let it turn your notes into a document reminding you to call the pathology department for the fourth time. The AI is not the oncologist. It is the tireless junior project manager assigned to the patient, because otherwise the patient is also the junior project manager.

Root for two zeros

A person wanted to download a NixOS ISO and ended up exploiting the Linux kernel on his Wi-Fi access point. Reasonable escalation.

The problem was unusually slippery. Large transfers over a TP-Link EAP770 would fail with SSH MAC errors or TLS "bad record mac" errors. TCP appeared satisfied. Packet captures eventually revealed why: A damaged TCP segment contained 64 wrong bytes in a row, but had a valid TCP checksum.

Normally corruption should make the checksum fail. Here, Qualcomm's traffic-offload system appears to have corrupted the payload and then recalculated the checksum over the corrupted data. TCP therefore received professionally certified garbage and passed it along. TLS and SSH, which authenticate data end to end, noticed that something had gone wrong.

The apparent cause was a DMA cache-coherency bug in Qualcomm's out-of-tree networking code. A dirty CPU cache line from a packet buffer's previous use could later be written over newly received data. The offload path would then checksum the result, converting memory corruption into valid network traffic.

The fix was wonderfully simple:

"echo 0 > /sys/sfe/l2_feature"

And one other zero written to another sysfs setting. Unfortunately both files were writable only by root. TP-Link's shell ran as user ID 1, there was no graphical setting, the device's original hardware version had received zero firmware updates, and TP-Link had declared it end of life. The owner knew exactly which two zeros his own machine needed, but the machine's security model would not let him type them.

So he used CVE-2022-0185, slab grooming, a key metadata leak, forged pipe buffers, an arbitrary physical-page write and an overwritten modprobe path to become root. He says this took about seventeen hours of "token gambling" with Opus 4.8. The access point stayed running, the offload was disabled and the corruption stopped.

There is a common concern that AI will make kernel exploitation easier. Here it made appliance maintenance possible. TP-Link had, the author notes, hardened its own software quite well: He found no useful vectors in its binaries, and it used whitelists extensively. The security worked. It successfully prevented the customer from repairing the unsupported product until he exploited the kernel.

2. Wanted to download an ISO, ended up writing a kernel exploit

blog.dfb.sh | 0xdeafbeef | 116 points | 59 comments | discussion

First seen: October 15, 2026 | Consecutive daily streak: 1 day

Analysis

The author traced recurring corruption in large LAN-to-Wi‑Fi transfers through an unsupported TP-Link EAP770 access point to a Qualcomm NSS/SFE/PPE offload driver bug: recycled packet buffers lack proper cache invalidation on the ARM-based IPQ9574, allowing stale cache lines to overwrite DMA-received data while checksums are later recalculated as valid. Because TP-Link had abandoned the device without firmware updates or a way to disable the faulty offload, the author used the known CVE-2022-0185 kernel vulnerability and pipe-buffer techniques to gain root, disable it, and restore reliable transfers at roughly an 8% speed cost. The incident highlights how platform-specific DMA and cache-coherency errors can silently corrupt data on non-coherent architectures while appearing correct on x86, and how closed firmware, vendor abandonment, and inaccessible configuration controls leave users to develop risky workarounds. The author also found similar potential driver bugs and separately suspects closed Qualcomm Wi‑Fi firmware of causing periodic transmission outages.
