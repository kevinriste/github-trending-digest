Hittite disclaimers

Here is a 3,400-year-old management memo:

Archaeologists excavating the ancient Hittite city of Samuha in central Turkey found 131 cuneiform tablets and fragments, including a letter from generals in western Anatolia asking King Tudhaliya II for more men, horses and chariots.

The generals warned that without reinforcements the army would be destroyed. They also had "the temerity to disclaim all responsibility for any upcoming failures if the reinforcements don't come."

Sure. The basic military situation was that the generals needed more resources, the king controlled the resources, and if the king did not provide them the generals wanted a written record that the resulting catastrophe was his fault. Technology changes, but the core purpose of business correspondence remains the same: I am writing to reiterate that, as discussed, your refusal to approve additional chariots may affect our ability to meet the western Anatolia deliverables.

This year's discoveries more than tripled the number of texts found at Samuha, making it the largest archive of Hittite-language documents discovered in the 21st century. The archive covers military logistics, royal life, religious practices, an epidemic of fever and bird divination. One tablet records the travels of bird diviners through different regions, including "the cities where they stayed and the birds they observed," in a diary-like format.

The Hittites had a large imperial bureaucracy in which some officials watched birds, some officials interpreted the birds, some officials recorded the interpretations, and some generals wrote memos explaining that they would not be held responsible if the king ignored them. This is approximately a modern government, except that the forecasting department used actual birds.

Bacterial strategy

An individual E. coli bacterium has no brain, but it can smell, remember and make tactical decisions. It senses chemicals through receptors and moves using a simple strategy: If conditions are improving, it swims forward in a "run." If they are getting worse, it spins into a random "tumble" and tries another direction.

This is already a recognizable investment process. Let your winners run; when the signal deteriorates, abandon the position and pick another direction randomly. Many more elaborate systems produce similar results.

The clever part is that E. coli does not merely react to the absolute amount of food nearby. It adapts to the current concentration as a new baseline, allowing it to remain sensitive to small improvements across five orders of magnitude. It stores this short-term memory through chemical modifications to its receptors. The article calls this a "simple chemical memory," though it seems a little rude to the bacterium. It has a functioning model of its environment, updates the model continuously and changes behavior based on the recent direction of the data.

The system also uses a constantly running cycle in which proteins are modified and demodified. This consumes energy and was once labeled "futile," but the cycle lets the cell change its internal signal rapidly. Keeping a costly process running continuously so that you can respond a little faster sounds less like futility than infrastructure.

So a single cell has sensors, memory, adaptive thresholds, signal amplification, exploration, exploitation and a low-latency decision system. It does not have a brain, an artificial neural network or a chief strategy officer. Possibly those are implementation details.

Analysis

The article explains how a single E. coli cell uses chemotaxis to find nutrients, combining chemical sensing, intracellular signaling, and motor control to bias its movement toward favorable conditions. Receptor proteins detect attractants, regulate phosphorylation of CheY, and thereby control whether flagellar motors produce forward runs or random tumbles. Receptor methylation provides short-term chemical memory and adaptation, allowing the cell to remain sensitive across a wide range of concentrations. The system illustrates how molecular interactions, feedback loops, and energy-consuming regulatory cycles can collectively produce sensing, decision-making, and behavior without a nervous system, offering a detailed model for understanding cellular computation and biological signaling.
