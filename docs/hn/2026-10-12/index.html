Type-safe HTML

Here is a reasonable software requirement:

"I don't want to deal with runtime template errors anymore."

Lukas Lalinsky has built zt, an HTML templating system that parses templates during zig build and transpiles them into Zig code. Template parameters are typed, dynamic values are escaped by default, URL attributes are checked for dangerous schemes, elements must be closed, and raw HTML requires an explicit escape hatch. If you write u.nmae instead of u.name, the compiler complains. All very civilized.

Also the generated code just writes directly to std.Io.Writer, with no allocations or intermediate state. The basic theory is that web development has spent 25 years accumulating layers of template engines and frontend frameworks, but if you combine arena allocators, compile-time metaprogramming and htmx, you can return to writing HTML in a low-level systems language without returning to string concatenation in a CGI script. Progress sometimes consists of going back to 2000 with a better compiler.

Here is the best part:

"As a side note, the main snippet above has a very non-obvious bug in error handling if you use the same pattern somewhere std.Io cancellation matters. I'll probably write a blog post about it."

Sure. The system will catch a misspelled field at compile time, escape your untrusted strings, reject dangerous URL schemes and prevent malformed markup. The introductory example contains a subtle cancellation bug. This is not really a criticism. It is just the natural frontier of type safety: Once the compiler eliminates all of the obvious errors, programmers are free to concentrate on much more sophisticated ones.

Rewrite history

The BBC reports that Deloitte Cyprus helped Roman Abramovich's advisers make hundreds of millions of dollars of assets disappear from company accounts and apparently advised on a tax-evasion scheme involving backdated documents.

In 2006, a new Cyprus transparency law threatened to expose $672 million of luxury property held by Abramovich's company Amiscan Investments, including homes, helicopters, private jets and yachts. So Abramovich's associates planned to transfer the assets to a parent company in the British Virgin Islands and backdate the transfer by three years. Deloitte reviewed the backdated documents and found them "adequate." When it signed off on the accounts, only $75,000 of assets remained.

Accounting is the science of accurately recording economic history, except that sometimes history has inconvenient disclosure requirements, in which case you can apparently revise it.

There was also Kravin Investments, another Abramovich company, which had almost $2 billion in the bank and was earning taxable interest. After a meeting with Deloitte, MeritServus, the corporate-services firm administering Abramovich's offshore companies, proposed changing the financial statements and accounting records for 2003 and 2004. It then created backdated documents showing that Kravin was merely holding the money for its offshore parent, so Kravin supposedly earned no taxable income from it.

Deloitte wrote that "certain changes will have to be made to the accounts" because of the backdated changes. Yes. If you alter the past, the accounts do need to be updated to reflect the new past. Otherwise they would be inaccurate.

Barrister Andrew Mitchell KC called the scheme "straightforward" tax evasion and said "it couldn't be more blatant." Abramovich's lawyers say he did not evade tax, did not know about or direct the falsification of documents, and was entitled to rely on professional advice. Deloitte Cyprus says it acts in compliance with applicable laws and cannot address the specific allegations because the documents are more than 20 years old and no longer accessible.

This is a useful professional-services structure. The client relies on the accountants because they are the experts. The accountants cannot comment because the records are old. And the records themselves say that the assets were somewhere else three years earlier. Everyone is relying on documentation, particularly the people who created it.

Zig Makes HTML Templates Type-Safe

lalinsky.com  ·  131 pts  ·  58 comments

Compile templates during `zig build`. `zt` checks parameters, escapes dynamic content, validates URLs, and writes directly to `std.Io.Writer`—a compact path to safer server-rendered applications.

Read →Share →
[ Analysis + ]

Technical Analysis

The article introduces **zt**, a Zig HTML templating library inspired by Templ for Go, designed for server-rendered applications built with the author’s Dusty and zio ecosystem. Templates are transpiled into Zig during `zig build`, enabling compile-time checking of parameters and expressions, direct allocation-free output to `std.Io.Writer`, and familiar HTML-oriented syntax with Zig control flow and reusable layouts. Dynamic content is escaped by default, URL schemes are checked, elements must be properly closed, and raw HTML requires explicit syntax, addressing common XSS and markup errors. The approach is important because it brings safer, high-performance templating to low-level Zig web development while supporting htmx or Datastar applications without requiring JavaScript-heavy frontends, though the project remains early and its syntax is still evolving.

Fast Storage Lets Postgres Breathe

clickhouse.com  ·  105 pts  ·  77 comments

In a 3.3-billion-row workload, local NVMe reached 16,030 transactions per second against 1,734 on baseline cloud storage. The benchmark makes an old systems lesson concrete: waiting on I/O can look like a database problem.

Read →Share →
[ Analysis + ]

Technical Analysis

The article summarizes a benchmark arguing that many PostgreSQL scaling problems—slow writes, variable read latency, delayed vacuuming, long checkpoints, and replication lag—can stem primarily from storage bottlenecks rather than PostgreSQL’s CPU processing. In a 3.3-billion-row random-update workload, local NVMe achieved a median 16,030 TPS versus 1,734 TPS on baseline 3,000-IOPS gp3 EBS, while also reducing transaction latency and speeding maintenance operations. The analysis attributes the gap mainly to faster data-page reads and WAL fsyncs, allowing NVMe-backed systems to spend more time doing useful work instead of waiting on I/O. Because local NVMe is ephemeral, the proposed production design combines it with synchronous replication across availability zones and independent object-storage backups plus continuous WAL archiving. The story is important because it frames storage selection and failure-recovery architecture as central decisions when PostgreSQL workloads reach large scale.

An iPad Runs Linux’s AMD Driver

github.com  ·  33 pts  ·  12 comments

LemonSeed Studio embeds upstream `amdgpu` and `amdkfd` inside an iPad app through PCIDriverKit. Add a Thunderbolt enclosure and a custom inference engine, and the tablet becomes an unlikely host for desktop-class GPU work.

Read →Share →
[ Analysis + ]

Technical Analysis

The project, LemonSeed Studio, is an iPad editor and IDE that runs local large-language-model inference on an AMD GPU in a Thunderbolt enclosure. Its notable technical achievement is embedding the unmodified upstream Linux `amdgpu` and `amdkfd` drivers inside the iPad app as a PCIDriverKit extension, rather than relying on a rewritten or vendor-specific driver. The app also bundles a custom inference engine and GPU telemetry tools, exposing AMD’s HSA runtime to the workload. This is significant because it demonstrates a way to bring desktop-class external GPU computing and on-device AI to iPadOS using largely upstream Linux graphics code, though it depends on specialized hardware and Apple driver-extension capabilities.

Show HN: PS2 games statically recompiled to WebAssembly

github.com  ·  41 pts  ·  12 comments  ·  discussion

Analysis

The project statically recompiles PlayStation 2 game executables into C++ and WebAssembly, pairing them with a reimplemented PS2 operating system and WebGPU-based hardware renderer so games run in browsers without a conventional emulator. Gran Turismo 4 and God of War II currently boot and play, with sound, video, saves, and performance near or above native PS2 frame rates on laptop hardware, while users must supply disc images they own. Its technical challenges include accurately reproducing the PS2’s unusual floating-point behavior, vector units, graphics pipeline, timing, I/O hardware, and browser threading and GPU constraints. The work is important because it demonstrates that complex console software can be ported through ahead-of-time recompilation and browser APIs, though expanding support remains game-specific and legally limited to user-provided copies.

Whatever became of holography? (2011)

americanscientist.org  ·  137 pts  ·  67 comments  ·  discussion

Analysis

The article revisits the promise of holography as a revolutionary form of three-dimensional imaging and explains why it did not become a mainstream display technology. Holograms require highly coherent light, precise optical setups, substantial data and fabrication costs, and often provide limited viewing angles or dim images, making practical consumer products difficult. Instead, holography found narrower commercial uses in security labels, measurement, microscopy, artistic displays, and specialized data-storage research. Its importance lies in showing how an impressive scientific principle can remain valuable while falling short of broad adoption because of engineering, economic, and usability constraints.
