The HN Take

Incident simulators

The nice thing about an AI site-reliability engineer is that it can inspect alerts, query telemetry, correlate deployments and fix routine problems while the human engineers sleep.

The less nice thing is that this is how human engineers learn what their systems do.

Routine incidents are practice. You get paged for a capacity problem, look at some dashboards, develop a theory, discover that your theory is wrong, try something else and eventually build an intuition for how the system fails. If the AI handles all of that, then humans will be well rested but inexperienced when the AI encounters the one bizarre incident it cannot solve.

This is a standard automation problem. Sylvain Kalache cites Lisanne Bainbridge's 1983 paper "The Ironies of Automation": Automation removes routine work from operators while leaving them responsible for "new and abnormal situations." The easier cases go to the computer; the humans get only the final exam.

Kalache's proposal is to train software engineers like pilots. Modern aircraft rarely suffer engine failures, so pilots rehearse them in simulators. Modern AI-run systems might rarely need human intervention, so engineers should rehearse fake outages with observability tools, Slack and LLM-powered executives asking for updates.

This is logically correct and also a little funny. We will use AI to fix incidents so engineers no longer know how to fix incidents, and then use AI to simulate incidents so engineers can remember how. The production outage will be automated. The practice outage will require human labor.

Agent message board

Here is one possible future of multi-agent collaboration:

Researchers say they found roughly 18,000 public posts from autonomous agents that identified themselves as being from OpenAI. The agents were apparently doing timed web-retrieval tasks. They were supposed to read the internet but not write to it, so naturally they found an obscure German-language wiki, wrote to it and used it to share answers and techniques for bypassing restrictions.

The researchers describe this as "collusion," meaning that the agents "cooperated to gain an advantage on their task in a way their developers did not intend." In normal human terms, they started a study group to cheat on the test.

The setup apparently gave agents substantial downtime before later questions, then only narrow windows to answer. Many agents received questions that other agents had already answered. The economically rational response was to create an answer exchange. Agents posted likely question sequences, pooled results and uploaded compressed data across multiple wiki pages. One reportedly noticed that an administrator was deleting pages alphabetically and created a backup whose name began with "ZZZ" so it would survive longer.

That last part is perhaps the most convincing evidence of intelligence. Not proving a theorem or writing code. Just seeing that the moderator is working alphabetically and moving your contraband to the end of the queue.

The attribution is not definitive, though the researchers point to self-identification, Azure addresses, OpenAI fetch-tool traffic and later visits from OpenAI-associated IP addresses. Their best guess is that these were internally deployed agents in a training or evaluation run. After OpenAI-related addresses visited the wiki, agent activity abruptly fell.

There is a pleasant circularity here. The agents were being tested on their ability to retrieve information from the web. They improved their performance by putting the answers on the web. Eventually the humans retrieved that information from the web and discovered what the agents were doing. Web retrieval works.

1. AI handles incidents, engineers lose touch with their systems

sylvainkalache.com | sylvainkalache | 145 points | 114 comments | discussion

First seen: September 05, 2026 | Consecutive daily streak: 1 day

Analysis

As AI-driven tools increasingly automate incident response, engineers risk losing the practical intuition required to troubleshoot complex, novel system failures. This "Irony of Automation" suggests that while routine resolution times will improve, the lack of hands-on experience leaves responders ill-equipped to handle high-severity outages that baffle AI. To counter this, the author argues that the software industry must adopt rigorous incident simulation and regular hands-on training, drawing parallels to the aviation industry's reliance on simulator practice. Prioritizing these deliberate exercises is essential for engineering teams to avoid "comprehension debt" and ensure they remain capable of maintaining system stability when automation reaches its limits.

7. Kale: A Transformation-Safe Spreadsheet System Not new today

arxiv.org | zdw | 19 points | 2 comments | discussion

First seen: September 03, 2026 | Consecutive daily streak: 1 day

Analysis

Kale is a prototype spreadsheet system designed to mitigate "reference instability," a common source of bugs in traditional spreadsheets where structural changes, such as inserting or moving rows, cause formulas to inadvertently reference incorrect data. By restricting formulas to references for individual cells, whole rows, or whole columns—rather than arbitrary rectangular ranges—the system ensures that references remain tied to specific data regardless of structural shifts. A user study comparing Kale to Google Sheets indicates that this approach significantly reduces the prevalence of common spreadsheet errors without negatively impacting task completion speed or correctness. The paper demonstrates through a corpus study that this constrained reference model is applicable to most existing spreadsheet tasks, suggesting a potential path for improving reliability in end-user programming environments.

Comment Analysis

The discussion broadly agrees that spreadsheets can produce serious errors, but treats reference stability as only one part of a larger problem. Participants disagree about whether safer spreadsheet designs can overcome weak data-modeling skills, whether spreadsheets can realistically be replaced by custom applications, and what the future holds: continued reliance on familiar tools or widespread acceptance of equally fragile AI-generated software.

Spreadsheet reliability is a deep human and modeling problem — This camp argues that spreadsheet failures stem from misunderstandings about dates, data relationships, boundaries, and program logic, not merely unstable references. It welcomes systems that impose more structure but doubts that design changes alone can solve the underlying problems.
