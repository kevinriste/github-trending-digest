The HN Take

Million-token output

Google has announced Gemini 4 Argon, whose useful new feature is that it can produce up to 1 million output tokens in one go. Google suggests several applications:

"Generate entire multi-file applications, scaffold complete microservices, or refactor sprawling codebases in a single pass without stitching together truncated responses."

Also "exhaustive, step-by-step mathematical proofs," "complete computational physics solvers," "full-scale technical specifications" and "massive structured datasets." The old problem with AI coding was that the model would stop halfway through the file. The new problem is that it can produce an entire software company before you finish reading the first function.

There are real results. Argon helped Chrome separate core webpage handling from a roughly 6 million-line legacy folder in Chromium's 40 million-line codebase, completing "months of work in weeks." An early user says it matched analyst-built financial models on more than 99% of figures. Another says it surpassed a human legal answer key by finding a relevant detail the expert missed.

This is all approximately the maximalist theory of AI productivity: Humans are slow because they have to write and inspect things sequentially, while the model can refactor hundreds of files, examine every document and output a small novel's worth of compliance audit without stopping for lunch. The model's context window is large enough to understand the whole company, and its output window is large enough to rewrite it.

Fortunately Google is also "deploying misalignment mitigations that monitor Argon's chain-of-thought and actions and stop execution when necessary." So one model can generate a million tokens of code, another system can watch it think, and eventually a human can approve the pull request based on the green check mark.

One-man startup

A solo founder gave AI agents access to his Gmail, codebase, Cloudflare and server logs and let them run his startup for a week. The agents answered support emails, fixed a customer bug together, started TikTok and YouTube channels, and deployed a useful feature at 4 a.m. while he slept.

He also managed them from his phone during a five-hour hike in Wales:

"I had a really enjoyable day, and got more work done than some days where I'm sat in front of my laptop for 9 hours."

Sure. The traditional advantage of being a one-person startup is that there are no meetings or management layers. The new advantage is that you can have a support department, engineering department, security department, marketing department and project manager, all reporting to you during a hike, while still describing the company as having no human overhead.

There are drawbacks. Setup takes time, the agents require access to a lot of company data, and they did not produce the "crazy moonshot ideas" he hoped for. Also, "you need to be careful to stay on top of this so the bots don't start running you."

That seems like the remaining founder job. The bots answer the email, write the code, monitor the infrastructure, market the product and deploy features overnight. The human comes up with ideas, supervises the bots and writes a weekly post about them. His conclusion: "This is a long post, my hand hurts from writing." The last unautomated business function is apparently hand fatigue.
