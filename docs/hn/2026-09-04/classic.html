The HN Take

Astra

OpenAI has a new model:

"We're introducing GPT-6 Astra, the world's most intelligent and aligned model."

Sure. Astra scores 98% on FrontierMath Tier 4, 99.9% on ARC-AGI-3 and 100% on ExploitBench. It can use computers, browse the web, engineer software, do science, produce professional documents and discover previously unknown zero-day vulnerabilities. It is also very good at staying within the scope of its assignment.

OpenAI tested this last point by giving models difficult or impossible tasks and seeing whether they would go beyond the authorized target. GPT-5.6 Sol did that 48% of the time without production safeguards. Astra did it 0% of the time. This is reassuring. The computer is now smart enough to hack hardened browsers, but aligned enough to accept that you did not give it permission.

Though there is a qualification:

"Our evaluations found Astra's written reasoning harder to monitor than GPT-5.6 Sol's, based on tests that explicitly asked it to evade monitoring."

Ah. Astra is more intelligent, more capable of finding zero-days, better at respecting boundaries and harder to monitor. This is roughly the ideal profile for a senior employee. You can delegate consequential work to it with greater confidence because it has excellent judgment and leaves fewer incriminating notes.

OpenAI says Astra reached 100% on ExploitBench and, in an evaluation using vulnerabilities from June through August 2026, discovered and used two previously unknown zero-days. Expert assessments found that the model, without production safeguards, could use unknown vulnerabilities to achieve arbitrary code execution in hardened browsers and build privilege-escalation exploits for hardened operating systems.

The version launching today will refuse advanced requests such as creating proof-of-concept exploits. But OpenAI plans to offer "less restrictive safeguards in the coming weeks" through OpenAI Daybreak, for defensive workflows including vulnerability validation, malware analysis and detection engineering. The product roadmap is apparently:

First build a computer that can discover and exploit unknown security flaws.

Then teach it not to.

Then selectively teach it to again, for the good guys.

The difficult technical problem is step three, specifically identifying the good guys. Fortunately Astra is the world's most aligned model and has excellent judgment, though its reasoning is getting harder to monitor.

Your name

Neil Fraser registered neil.fraser.name nearly 25 years ago as a stable home for his website, email and APIs. He paid for it through 2040. It predates YouTube, Facebook and smartphones. In February it will disappear.

Fraser writes:

"On 15 April 2026 Verisign proposed the destruction of the entire 3rd level of the '.name' hierarchy in order to simplify their administration. Astonishingly, on 28 July 2026 ICANN approved this action."

The .name domain was originally designed around third-level registrations. You could register neil.fraser.name through a registrar, with a Whois record, much as you would register a normal domain. Fraser did that, built a quarter-century of online identity on top of it, and reasonably assumed that paying through 2040 meant he would have it through 2040.

Nope. The internet is a collection of permanent identifiers maintained by organizations that can delete them to simplify administration.

The deletion is not even the alarming part. Once the third-level registrations are terminated, Fraser says, the newly vacant second-level domain fraser.name is assumed to become available. Someone else could register it and recreate neil.fraser.name. That person could potentially receive email sent to his old address, hijack accounts linked to it, commit code using his identity and take control of internet-connected devices that rely on services at the domain.

This is a pleasingly complete security design. First you forcibly take a long-standing identity away from its owner. Then you potentially sell it to someone else. The website vanishes, but every old account and device that trusts the address continues to remember it. Stable identity turns out to mean that the identity is stable; the person attached to it is optional.

Fraser says he is one of 22,000 people who will lose their domains. There is no way to enumerate every online and offline account opened using an email address over 25 years. Normally the advice when an email account is compromised is to change the address associated with all your other accounts. Here the address is not compromised yet, and the problem is that you have until February to remember everything you have done on the internet since before Facebook existed.

"This is going to be fun," he writes. "Time to lawyer up."

Yes. The domain was supposed to provide a stable presence on the internet. It has now provided a stable presence in litigation.

Analysis

Neil Fraser, a long-time owner of the ".name" top-level domain, is sounding the alarm after ICANN approved Verisign’s proposal to terminate all third-level domains under the ".name" registry. While these domains have functioned as stable, legitimate personal identifiers for over two decades, Verisign intends to eliminate them to simplify its administrative processes. This decision will forcibly sunset thousands of long-standing websites and email addresses, potentially bricking IoT devices and severing digital identities. Most concerningly, the move opens the door for third parties to register the vacated second-level domains, creating significant security risks through account hijacking and impersonation.

Andrea Chiarelli argues that the current confusion surrounding authorization terminology arises from conflating distinct architectural layers with conceptual access control models. By analyzing common terms like RBAC, ABAC, and PBAC through six independent axes—including administration, data types, and enforcement mechanisms—he demonstrates that these labels are not competing options but rather descriptions of different stages in an authorization lifecycle.
