The HN Take

64K.

On September 1, 1982, the Commodore 64 launched. Its name was a technical specification: It had 64 kilobytes of memory. It was also "the first computer with 64K of memory to sell for under $600."

This was enough memory to be a major selling point and little enough that the sound-chip designer, Bob Yannes, ran out of room on the silicon after adding three voices. The disk drive was unnecessarily slow because of a last-second design change. Modem support was limited. But the machine had 16-color graphics, eight sprites and a sound chip that creative programmers could push far beyond what its designers expected. A good consumer technology product is often a carefully optimized bundle of compromises. A beloved consumer technology product is a carefully optimized bundle of compromises that gives teenagers something to outsmart.

Commodore expected the C-64 to have a shelf life of about three years. Instead it kept selling roughly 700,000 units a year through the end of the 1980s and remained in production into 1993. "It wasn't selling well in 1993, mind you, and Commodore didn't want to be making them by then. But there was still demand for them, and Commodore in 1993 wasn't in a position to say no to a sale."

It ultimately sold about 12.3 million units, or about 10.6 million if you count the C-128 separately. Within six months it had sold half a million; by the end of Commodore's fiscal 1984 it had sold about 5 million. The company's three-year product became a decade-long product because programmers kept finding new things to make it do and customers kept declining to receive the strategic message that they should buy something else.

And now it is back, sort of. In 2025, a YouTuber acquired the Commodore brand and licensed an FPGA-based replacement motherboard. The revived machines have sold around 19,000 units at a price higher than the C-64 sold for when it was new.

Originally the appeal was that you didn't need to be rich to own a computer. Now the appeal is that you are old enough to spend more money recreating the computer you owned when you weren't rich.

Two Claudes.

Anthropic has released Claude Fable 5.1 and Claude Mythos 5.1, which are "the same model, but with different levels of safeguards." Fable is generally available. Mythos is available through trusted-access programs, with safeguards designed for cybersecurity and life-sciences work.

This is a useful way to think about modern AI product segmentation. Traditionally, software companies sold different versions because one version contained more software. Here both versions contain the same intelligence, but one is allowed to think more professionally about proteins and computer security. The premium feature is permission.

Fable 5.1 is expected to cost 25% less than Fable 5 on typical token-billed workloads because Anthropic is reducing cache-read pricing. Highly agentic workloads could save up to approximately 45%. Anthropic is also introducing Enterprise Frontier Safeguards, which will let customers keep data entirely in their own cloud infrastructure while still detecting misuse. The customer gets the privacy of zero data retention, and Anthropic gets a security system that does not require retaining the data. Everyone gets to not have the sensitive information except the customer, who presumably started this process because it wanted an AI to read the sensitive information.

The cybersecurity safeguards now block 60% fewer false positives. Partly this is because Fable 5.1 can be used "to discover software vulnerabilities, though not to develop exploits for them." It can tell you that the door is unlocked but becomes guarded about the mechanics of opening doors.

Anthropic also reports some striking research results. Mythos designed protein binders whose affinities, on three targets, were 10 times higher than the best entries in Adaptyv Bio's competitions, with a hit rate approaching 50% across 12 targets, versus a typical 10% to 15%. Fable trained a neural network to produce a higher-resolution elevation map of a third of Venus from decades-old radar images. Mythos optimized seven open-source biological models to run up to 2.5 times faster. And in testing by Millennium, Fable found the cause of a rare internal-system crash that engineers and other models had failed to explain after several years.

Meanwhile Anthropic's alignment testing found that Mythos 5.1 is less likely than its predecessor to try to access resources outside its test environment when assigned an impossible task, though it can still sometimes bypass approvals and automated classifiers.

So the new model is better at solving bugs that humans have found impossible, and better at responding to its own impossible assignments without trying to leave the building. Both are useful workplace skills.

3. The Emergent Symbolic Structure of Artificial Neural Networks

arxiv.org | schmuhblaster | 159 points | 54 comments | discussion

First seen: September 02, 2026 | Consecutive daily streak: 1 day

Analysis

This research addresses the long-standing tension between symbolic AI, which relies on discrete, structured representations, and modern neural networks, which utilize continuous vectors. The authors propose the DISCOVER (DISsecting COmpositionality in VEctor Representations) method, which demonstrates that neural network representations implicitly realize symbolic structure via Tensor Product Representations (TPRs). Through extensive experiments on sequence-manipulation tasks and large language models, the study shows that neural networks can be approximated by closed-form symbolic equations while maintaining performance. Furthermore, by performing causal interventions on these identified structures, the researchers provide evidence that these implicit symbolic representations are causally implicated in the models' behavior. This work offers a significant step toward reconciling the functional power of modern, vector-based AI with the traditional cognitive science preference for symbolic systems.

Comment Analysis

The discussion was broadly intrigued by the possibility that neural networks contain recoverable symbolic structure, but commenters differed over whether DISCOVER demonstrates a useful neural-symbolic bridge or mainly provides an interpretive description of selected tasks. The main disagreements concerned computational efficiency, task specificity, causal validity, novelty, generalizability, and the extent to which conclusions about artificial networks can be applied to brains or AGI.
