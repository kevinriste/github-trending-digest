Electoral randomness

Here is some evidence of algorithmic political favoritism:

Sleeping Giants Brasil and CTRL+Z have asked Brazil's Superior Electoral Court to force Meta to explain why searches on Instagram and WhatsApp allegedly favor Sen. Flavio Bolsonaro over President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva. A WhatsApp GIF search for "brasil" returned Bolsonaro's image, ticket number and running mate first on three accounts. Searching "ladrao," or thief, returned a caricature of Lula in prison clothes and another GIF saying "Supporting the Workers' Party is a mental illness."

Sure. Search rankings matter, and if searching for Brazil produces campaign material for one candidate while searching for thief produces campaign material attacking the other, that seems like the sort of thing an electoral court might want Meta to explain.

There is also this:

The organizations included a test of Meta AI, the artificial intelligence tool offered to WhatsApp users. According to the petition, two people asked the system to pick a number between 1 and 25. They gave no instructions and made no reference to the election.

In one attempt, the AI answered 13. In the other, it answered 22. Those are the party ballot numbers of Lula and Flavio Bolsonaro, respectively.

"The groups question the coincidence."

I suppose if Meta AI had answered 22 twice, that would look like Bolsonaro favoritism. If it had answered 13 twice, that would look like Lula favoritism. Instead it picked each candidate's number once, which sounds like exquisitely balanced electoral treatment. The court ordered Meta to apply identical criteria to both campaigns, and the random-number generator apparently took that instruction literally.

Still, Meta faces a daily fine of R$500,000, potentially rising to R$1 million, and the groups want it to explain the parameters used to generate the answers. Presumably Meta's explanation will be "we asked the artificial intelligence to pick a number and it picked a number," though in election litigation that is less a defense than the beginning of discovery.

Rewrite the compiler

The traditional advice about software rewrites is that you should not do them. Existing systems contain years of accumulated knowledge, including many ugly solutions to problems that you have forgotten exist. If your compiler is slow, you should profile it and improve the bottlenecks, not casually write another compiler.

But that advice was developed under what Dan Luu calls a "pre-agentic cost model":

Ten years ago, the kind of person who implemented a new compiler because their compiler was slow was the kind of person who started programming a new 3d game engine when they wanted to make an indie game, i.e., the kind of person who never ships anything.

Now, he argues, AI coding agents have lowered the cost of ambitious software enough that casually writing another compiler might be reasonable. He built a regex engine, a faster ripgrep fork and a Rust interpreter in "a low single-digit number of hours" of his own time. Michael Malis's pgrust project, an attempted Rust reimplementation of PostgreSQL, has tested PostgreSQL thoroughly enough to uncover numerical, caching, cryptographic, overflow and memory-safety bugs.

This does not mean software is solved. Luu writes that more capable models let him attempt harder projects, so he actually gets farther from the experience where "it just works." The models can implement more than they can reliably verify, leaving the human to check increasingly ambitious piles of code.

Still, the new programming advice might be: Don't rewrite the database unless you can do it over the weekend and accidentally improve the original database along the way. Then, sure, why not.

3. Cerebras Deploys Trillion-Parameter AI Models

cerebras.ai | gmays | 185 points | 61 comments | discussion

First seen: October 25, 2026 | Consecutive daily streak: 1 day

Analysis

Cerebras describes how it deploys trillion-parameter models by distributing quantized model layers across multiple wafer-scale WSE-3T chips, each with 44 GB of on-chip SRAM. Using a roughly 3-trillion-parameter Kimi K3 example, it maps dense layers one per wafer to reserve space for KV caches and linear-attention layers two per wafer, requiring about 59 wafers overall. The wafers form a pipeline that passes only activations over RDMA, while concurrent requests and micro-batches keep different stages busy, trading latency against throughput. The approach is important because it offers a concrete path to serving models larger than any single accelerator’s memory while highlighting memory capacity, interconnect overhead, concurrency, and power efficiency as key deployment constraints.

Analysis

The story documents how katsuobushi, or Japanese dried bonito, is produced at Kanesa Katsuobushi Shoten, a workshop founded in 1882. 6. The Goose Programming Language

aardappel.github.io | bobbydigitales | 423 points | 258 comments | discussion

First seen: October 25, 2026 | Consecutive daily streak: 1 day

Analysis

Goose is a new memory-safe systems programming language that eliminates heap allocation, garbage collection, reference counting, and lifetime annotations by storing dynamically sized values inline on compiler-managed data stacks. It uses static lifetime checks, stable references, contiguous data layouts, compact relative links, in-place construction, and isolated message-passing threads, while compiling to C and interoperating directly with C libraries. Its authors report substantially better speed and memory usage than idiomatic and even hand-optimized C++ and safe Rust across 16 benchmarks, though these claims require independent validation. The trade-offs include whole-program compilation, nonescaping function values, constrained recursive and variable-size data structures, and the need for programmers to reason about ownership and scope, making Goose notable as an experimental alternative for performance-sensitive systems software.

8. Cannabis legalisation and cardiovascular hospitalisations in the USA

bmjopen.bmj.com | gumby | 9 points | 6 comments | discussion

First seen: October 25, 2026 | Consecutive daily streak: 1 day

Analysis

A study using more than 50 million US hospital discharges examined whether recreational cannabis legalisation was associated with cardiovascular hospitalisations, comparing the Western region after sales began in 2014 with other regions before 2018. In an adjusted difference-in-differences analysis, each additional year of legal sales correlated with hospitalisation increases of about 2.3% for atrial fibrillation, 3.7% for heart failure and 3.1% for myocardial infarction, while stroke, venous thromboembolism and ventricular tachycardia were not statistically significant. Cannabis-related admissions rose as expected, whereas opioid-related admissions and urinary-stone hospitalisations—negative controls—did not change significantly. The findings suggest potential cardiovascular and healthcare-use consequences of legalisation, but the regional observational design remains vulnerable to population-level trends and cannot establish causation.
