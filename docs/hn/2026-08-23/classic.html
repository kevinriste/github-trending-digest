Silicon extraction

Here is an inconvenient way to inspect a processor:

When the author removed a heatsink from an Athlon XP, "a piece of the CPU" remained glued to it. Not a metaphorical piece, not a bit of thermal compound, but a substantial chunk of silicon. The interesting part is that the processor "worked just fine until a good chunk of it came off."

Sure. Computers are often described as delicate machines, but normally this means that a microscopic transistor has failed somewhere inside them. This Athlon apparently had a long micro-crack through its exposed silicon and continued operating normally, right up until someone opened the case and physically extracted part of the processor. The CPU was tolerant of structural damage but not of being disassembled into multiple CPUs.

Around 2000, Intel and AMD used flip-chip PGA packaging, exposing the silicon to improve cooling as processor power consumption climbed. This worked, thermally. Mechanically it introduced the possibility that installing or removing a heatsink would become a semiconductor fabrication process. Apply the pressure unevenly and you could create several smaller chips from one larger chip, though generally not in a way that increased computing capacity.

The industry moved toward lidded processors, which kept good cooling while making the silicon much harder to damage. Intel's pin-less LGA processors are particularly sturdy, the author notes, "although the weak spot simply moved to the motherboard socket instead."

Right. There is a general engineering principle here. If an expensive component has a fragile exposed surface, put a protective lid on it. Then strengthen the package. Then remove the vulnerable pins. Eventually the processor becomes extremely robust, and all you have done is arrange for the motherboard to break instead. The fragility has not disappeared. It has been transferred to a larger and less convenient component.

Assembly by AI

The conventional story about artificial intelligence and programming is that AI will let everyone write software without understanding code. Here is the opposite version:

"Now, with the use of AI, it's easier than ever to write a JIT compiler with fast compile times by directly targeting assembly."

The author built a just-in-time compiler for pgrust that compiles code in about 5 microseconds, fast enough to compile every SQL query. The technique in the article is "copy-and-patch": You prepare small ARM64 machine-code templates, called stencils, and fill in values such as characters, branch locations and addresses at runtime. String the stencils together, put the resulting instructions into executable memory, and you have generated a function.

Historically the appeal of higher-level programming languages was that humans did not want to write assembly. Compilers would handle the obscure processor-specific details, humans would write comprehensible code, and everyone would be happier. But general-purpose compiler infrastructure can take long enough to run that it limits when JIT compilation is worthwhile. If you want compilation in 5 microseconds, it helps to skip the large compiler and emit machine instructions directly.

This used to be a "black art," because someone had to know the assembly. Now the AI can help. Thus one effect of making programming easier is that programmers can choose much harder programming. You no longer need to remain at a high level of abstraction merely because descending into raw ARM64 opcodes would consume your life. You can ask a language model to accompany you down there.

The future of accessible software development is apparently hexadecimal constants like "0xAA0103E2." Programming is solved now, so naturally everyone can move on to writing machine code.

Analysis

The author recounts a hardware failure where an Athlon XP processor was physically damaged during a standard heatsink removal, resulting in a large chunk of the silicon core breaking off. This incident highlights the inherent fragility of early 2000s flip-chip PGA packaging, which featured exposed silicon dies that were highly susceptible to cracking under uneven pressure during installation or maintenance. Both AMD and Intel eventually transitioned away from this design in favor of lidded processors, which provided better mechanical protection while maintaining necessary thermal performance. Ultimately, the story serves as a reminder of the significant physical engineering challenges manufacturers faced as power consumption and heat output escalated in early consumer CPUs.
