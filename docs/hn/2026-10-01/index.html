Open DLSS

Here is an open-source implementation of Nvidia's latest neural rendering system:

OpenDLSS-NR is "a Vulkan reimplementation of NVIDIA's DLSS 5 Neural Rendering network, bit-exact against the original."

Not merely image-exact. The repository says that all 75 intermediate block boundaries match "byte for byte." There is also a browser implementation that produces "the same bytes" using WebGPU, without FP8 or tensor cores.

Great. There is one small qualification:

"You supply the weights."

The repository contains the machinery for running Nvidia's 71-block neural rendering network, documentation of the complete graph, implementations of the kernels, a demo renderer, benchmarking tools and an elaborate system for proving that its results match Nvidia's. It does not contain the 141 MiB of weights, instructions for obtaining them or the reference captures used to demonstrate parity. It is an open implementation of a model that you have to bring from home.

This is not necessarily unreasonable. The interesting technical accomplishment is reconstructing the system closely enough that every intermediate matches, despite implementing it through Vulkan and then independently in a browser. Also the repository is careful to say that it contains "no NVIDIA software, weights, headers, or instructions for obtaining them." If you happen to possess the weights through whatever properly licensed process applies to you, congratulations, here is an exact alternative implementation. If you do not, you can admire the kernel scheduling.

There is another sense in which "OpenDLSS" is relative. The fast implementation requires Windows, a recent Nvidia GPU and several Nvidia-specific Vulkan extensions, and it launches generated PTX kernels on Nvidia tensor cores. You have escaped Nvidia's software implementation by writing a new implementation that runs on Nvidia's hardware through Nvidia's extensions. Freedom comes in layers.

Still, the browser version is genuinely portable in spirit. It runs the same network without tensor cores, FP8, kernel fusion or launch chaining. It takes 72 milliseconds at 512 by 512, versus 2.7 milliseconds for the optimized implementation. The good news is that the exactness is in the specification, not the hardware. The bad news is that the speed remains rather attached to the hardware.

Argon

Google announced Gemini 4 Argon, its new frontier model, and is initially releasing it to "a set of trusted cyber defenders" while it works on making the model safe enough for everyone else.

The trusted cyber defenders will receive Argon "without cyber guardrails so they can leverage its full frontier-level cybersecurity defense capabilities." This is a sensible arrangement. The model is powerful enough at cybersecurity that Google does not yet want to release it broadly, so the first thing Google will do is remove the cybersecurity safeguards and give it to a small group of people. They are the good people, is the important part.

Google says Argon can "autonomously find, validate, and patch critical software vulnerabilities." Wiz used it to find a critical vulnerability in healthcare software that previous frontier models had missed. On Wiz's black-box penetration-testing benchmark, it was better than Google's earlier cyber model at discovering attack surfaces, finding vulnerabilities and producing proof-of-concept evidence. Those are useful defensive skills. They are also, inconveniently, the same skills you would request if you replaced the word "defensive" with another word.

This is the general dual-use problem of cybersecurity AI. A model that cannot locate and demonstrate a vulnerability is not especially useful for fixing it. A model that can locate and demonstrate a vulnerability is useful for other things. The safety solution is therefore to identify trustworthy users, remove the guardrails for them and strengthen the guardrails before giving the model to everyone else. Cybersecurity becomes a know-your-customer business.

Argon also has a one-million-token output limit, up from 64,000. Google says that letting the model "generate hundreds of thousands of tokens in a single trajectory" gives it more room to solve difficult problems. At the introductory output price of $10 per million tokens, the full million-token monologue costs $10. You can now ask a computer to think about one problem for an extraordinary length of time at approximately the price of lunch, though reading what it thought may consume the rest of your week.

Internally, Google says Argon agents found memory optimizations that freed more than 300 TiB across its data centers, with estimated total savings of 500 TiB to 1 PiB. They are also migrating C and C++ codebases to Rust, including work spanning more than 800,000 lines for the Fuchsia Zircon kernel. This is probably the cleanest economic case for long-horizon agents: Give the model an enormous, tedious engineering project that humans would prefer not to do, let it run tools and experiments for a long time, and then have humans rigorously audit the result.

The model is helping Google save data-center memory, rewrite Google's code and defend Google's systems before Google sells it to everyone else. A frontier model's first customer is increasingly the company that trained it. If it can reduce Google's own costs enough, charging outsiders is almost a pleasant side business.

DLSS Rendering, Rebuilt in Vulkan

github.com  ·  95 pts  ·  54 comments

OpenDLSS-NR recreates Nvidia’s neural rendering network in portable Vulkan code, claiming byte-for-byte agreement across its 71-block FP8 model. The implementation opens the execution path to inspection while leaving model weights, licensing, and demanding hardware outside the box.

Read →Share →
[ Analysis + ]

Technical Analysis

OpenDLSS-NR is an MIT-licensed project that reimplements Nvidia’s DLSS 5 Neural Rendering network in Vulkan, claiming byte-for-byte agreement with the 71-block FP8 Swin/ViT model, including intermediate activations. Unlike DLSS Super Resolution, the network operates at the input resolution, using a rendered frame, noise, temporal history, and conditioning data to generate detail and adjust tone and structure. It includes optimized NVIDIA GPU kernels, a slower independent WebGPU browser implementation, a demo renderer, and tools for performance testing and parity verification, but users must supply compatible model weights. The project is significant because it makes the network’s execution publicly inspectable and portable, while remaining constrained by proprietary model data, demanding hardware and driver requirements, and unclear licensing around any weights obtained elsewhere.

How Iron Unmade the Bronze Age Order

worksinprogress.news  ·  255 pts  ·  161 comments

The collapse of the Bronze Age was not one catastrophe but a change in the economics of power. As iron drew weapons and tools away from scarce, tightly controlled tin routes, the material basis of empire began to fragment.

Read →Share →
[ Analysis + ]

Technical Analysis

The article argues that the Bronze Age collapse was not caused solely by drought, invasions, famine, or revolt, but was amplified by the spread of iron metallurgy. Bronze depended on scarce, long-distance tin supplies that powerful states could control, giving imperial centers an advantage in equipping armies; iron, though technically more difficult to produce and not inherently superior as a material, could be smelted from abundant local ores. This decentralized access to weapons weakened the military and political advantages of large empires, contributing to prolonged fragmentation and instability after their collapse. The broader lesson is that technologies that change the cost and accessibility of force can reshape institutions and power structures, much as firearms, drones, or potentially AI may do today.

The Brain Computes in Traveling Waves

quantamagazine.org  ·  192 pts  ·  74 comments

Intracranial recordings reveal planar, source, sink, and spiral waves moving through the brain during memory tasks.

Rotating patterns appeared especially often during navigation and recall, suggesting a dynamic coordination system whose computational role remains to be established.

Read →Share →
[ Analysis + ]

Technical Analysis

The article examines new evidence that electrical activity in the brain travels as complex waves—including planar, source, sink, and spiral patterns—rather than merely simple oscillations. Using high-resolution intracranial recordings from epilepsy patients, researchers found that different memory tasks were associated with distinct wave structures, with rotating waves appearing more often during spatial navigation and recall. Scientists propose that these patterns may dynamically coordinate brain regions, regulate neuronal excitability, and support prediction and cognition faster than anatomical connections can change. The findings are important because they could reshape how researchers understand real-time information processing, although it remains unsettled whether the waves actively perform computations or simply reflect underlying synaptic activity.
