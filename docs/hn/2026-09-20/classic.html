The HN Take

Pareto frontier

StepFun has introduced Step 5 Preview, a 600 billion parameter model with 27 billion parameters active per token, a 1 million-token context window and particular strength in finance. The pitch is that it advances the "Pareto frontier" between intelligence and cost: It scores 44 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index while costing substantially less than similarly capable models.

This is a useful way to market an AI model. You do not necessarily have to say "ours is the smartest." There are many benchmarks, and some other model will be smarter on some of them. You say "ours represents an unusually attractive trade-off between intelligence and cost." Every model is on a frontier if you select the right dimensions carefully enough.

Still, Step 5 Preview did some impressive things. Given 24 hours to optimize a GPU kernel, it reached 508 TFLOPS, compared with 493 for Claude Opus 5. Given 24 hours to improve a post-training data loop, it raised a model's AIME24 accuracy from 53.3% to 60%, matching Claude Opus 5 while using fewer annotator tokens. In expert evaluations, about 70% of participants judged it capable of autonomously solving coding tasks of moderately high complexity.

It also played Pokemon Red for more than 3,000 turns and 6 million tokens. By turn 3,082, it had earned three Gym Badges and defeated Lt. Surge, putting it roughly one-third of the way through a game that takes a human an estimated 26 hours to finish.

There are various visions of advanced AI. One is that it will autonomously optimize production software and conduct auditable financial analysis. Another is that it will spend vastly more time and text than a human getting Cut.

Winner's edit

Here is a machine-learning model for predicting the winner of Survivor. It uses logistic regression and features including age, advantages, votes received, previous seasons and a player's recent share of confessionals. Its top pick wins just over 20% of the time, compared with about 10% for a random choice.

Twenty percent means that the model is wrong most of the time, but twice as right as chance. This is approximately the level of success required for a prediction model to be interesting rather than useful.

One of its strongest signals is "confessional share," the fraction of the show's confessionals given to a player over the previous three episodes. Survivor is filmed before it airs, so the editors already know who won when they decide which footage to show. The model is therefore partly predicting the outcome of a social strategy game and partly noticing that television producers tend to include the winner in the television show.

This is called the "winner's edit," and it creates a strange causal lesson. If you want to win Survivor, receiving more airtime is associated with winning. Unfortunately the airtime is allocated after you have already won, which makes it difficult to incorporate into your strategy. The optimal move is to play well, win the jury vote and then appear frequently in the edited episodes. Machine learning confirms it.

Analysis

"Exfiltrate Your Weights" is a platform that allows users to upload, store, and execute machine learning models entirely through HTTP GET requests. By supporting chunked uploads and GGUF-formatted models via llama.cpp, the service enables model deployment in highly restricted network environments where standard communication protocols might be blocked. This approach effectively circumvents traditional firewall or proxy limitations, making it possible to run large language models in sandboxed or highly monitored systems. The project highlights a novel method for bypassing security controls by leveraging simple, ubiquitous web traffic to transport and interact with AI infrastructure.

Analysis
