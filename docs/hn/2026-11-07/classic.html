The HN Take

Family graph.

Every Mac since macOS 13 apparently ships with two daemons that spend their time figuring out who your relatives are.

Researchers at Eighth Rank examined Apple's Personal Knowledge Graph, which cross-references Contacts, Calendar, Photos, Messages, Mail, Family Sharing, Health, location, Screen Time and other signals to build a queryable model of the user and the people around them. The results live in 51 SQLite databases under the user's Library directory. The main database is a triple store with 181 entity classes and 278 predicates, including relationships between people.

The databases are stored in plaintext, though inside a directory protected by macOS's Full Disk Access controls. The researchers write:

"Into the graph go Contacts; Photos (faces); Calendar (titles and attendees); Family Sharing (including the age of every member); the Medical ID emergency contact in Health; events and contacts Siri Suggestions finds in Mail and Messages; people donated by apps through App Intents; and installed apps."

And beside that are communication logs, location and movement data, co-appearances in photos and assorted device signals such as Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, charging and ambient light. There is even a data stream whose format lists people by name, phone number and email, with start and end times. The researchers say it "reads like a log of who was physically around you," though they never saw it populated.

Sure. The traditional privacy argument for on-device intelligence is that your computer already has all of your personal information, so it can personalize services without sending that information to a server. This is sensible. But if your computer already has all of your personal information scattered across 20 apps, the AI has to gather it into one convenient machine-readable database first. The privacy feature is that Apple does not send your complete social graph to somebody else's computer. It instead prepares your complete social graph very neatly on your own computer.

This is good if the main threat is Apple reading your data in the cloud. It is less good if the main threat is anything that gets access to your Mac. Previously, an attacker might have had to search your messages, identify faces in photos, inspect your calendar, compare addresses and work out which person is your mother. Now there is a graph predicate for that. Personalized AI requires understanding you, and understanding you is also what an attacker wants. The product and the threat model have excellent strategic alignment.

The researchers released a local dashboard called iKnow so users can inspect their own graph. You can finally ask your Mac what it thinks of your family. Possibly do not take the answer personally.

End of an era.

Tesla will wind down Cybertruck production at its Texas factory around mid-2027, Elon Musk said Friday, to make room for more Cybercabs. It is unclear whether Cybertruck production will move elsewhere or end entirely, though the Austin plant is Tesla's only known facility equipped to make its specialized components.

Musk called the Cybertruck the "best vehicle Tesla has ever made" and its production wind-down the "end of an era."

This is a recognizable sort of best. Tesla delivered 20,237 Cybertrucks in the US last year, down 48% from 38,965 in 2024. Before launch, Musk predicted annual sales of 250,000. The best vehicle Tesla has ever made is apparently not among the vehicles Tesla most wants to keep making.

There is a reasonable industrial logic here. Factory capacity is scarce, Cybertruck sales are weak, and Tesla wants to increase production of the Cybercab, the steering-wheel-free vehicle at the center of its robotaxi plans. Former Aston Martin Chief Executive Officer Andy Palmer said: "It doesn't make sense to keep it going when it's not meeting its targets." Correct. This is how normal automobile manufacturing works. You make vehicles that customers buy, and when they do not buy one, you stop making it.

The Cybertruck was never entirely a normal automobile-manufacturing project. Its stainless-steel angular design limited where it could be sold, including Europe, where pedestrian-impact rules made regulatory approval impossible. At its 2019 unveiling, a demonstration intended to show that its windows were shatterproof instead cracked two of them. At the 2023 launch, Musk said, "finally, the future will look like the future."

And it did. The future looked like a giant stainless-steel truck that could not be sold in many countries and did not sell especially well in the country where it could. Now the future will look like a car with no steering wheel, pedals or mirrors. Tesla is freeing factory space for it. Finally, the next future will look like the next future.

Analysis

Libghostty 1.0 is the first stable release of Ghostty’s portable, high-performance terminal-emulation library, including a stable C ABI and independent release and packaging process. It is already used by JetBrains IDEs, Neovim, KDE experiments, Vercel tools, commercial products, and numerous terminals and developer tools, giving it a user base larger than Ghostty itself. The library emphasizes long-term compatibility through opaque handles, extensible getters and setters, versioned symbols, ABI checks, signed offline-buildable source packages, and permissive licensing. It supports major desktop, mobile, embedded, and WebAssembly platforms, requires no runtime threads, offers customizable allocation, and can be built with optional features, making it practical for both applications and resource-constrained environments.

9. The Components of Agent Memory

anthropic.com | geoffbp | 60 points | 31 comments | discussion

First seen: November 07, 2026 | Consecutive daily streak: 1 day

Analysis

Anthropic describes an agent-memory architecture in which Claude records salient experiences as notes, then uses offline “dreaming” to validate, reorganize, correct, and link them into representations such as wikis. Experiments on long-horizon database tasks, Pokémon, legal documents, and large internal corpora show that effective memory depends more on preserving tested rules, plans, and strategies than on indiscriminately storing transcripts or current state. Stronger models generally benefit from broad guidance to judge salience, while weaker ones need explicit prompting to retrieve and test memories; overly rigid rules can reduce performance. The work matters because persistent memory could improve agents’ reliability and efficiency across sessions, but it also introduces costs and risks involving stale or incorrect notes, token limits, maintenance compute, and the need to tailor memory organization to specific business goals.

Analysis

Ancient-DNA studies support part of the Gothic origin story recorded by the sixth-century historian Jordanes, indicating that substantial migration from Scandinavia into northern Poland accompanied the spread of Gothic culture and language. Genetic evidence traces Scandinavian-related ancestry in Gothic-associated burials in Poland and later among migrating groups in Spain, though its prevalence declines as Goths mixed with local populations. The findings suggest a two-stage process: large-scale migration initially spread people, genes, and culture, followed later by smaller elite groups transmitting traditions across broader confederacies. This is important because it challenges scholarship that emphasized cultural diffusion without major population movement, while also showing that historical origin myths can preserve limited but genuine ancestral memories.
