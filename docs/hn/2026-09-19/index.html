AI poster diversification

The problem with AI-generated event posters is apparently not that AI can make only one sort of poster. It is that, unless instructed otherwise, AI will make the same sort of poster every time: bunting, florals, pastel colors, generalized spring-fayre cheerfulness. The machines have absorbed the whole history of graphic design and concluded that the Leamington Beer Festival wants bunting.

John Hartnup tried asking ChatGPT for something else. His initial request for a "clean, unfussy, bright layout" still produced the familiar AI look, so he told it to treat that poster as "what not to do." It came back with a Bauhaus-influenced geometric design and then helpfully offered a menu of other possibilities: Swiss Style, risograph, brutalism, Memphis, Japanese minimalism, 90s rave flyer, wayfinding signage, etc.

This is useful. The AI is not just a graphic designer; it is also the person who tells you what words to use when talking to the graphic designer. You say "not the usual AI stuff," it makes something different, you ask "what is that called," and it teaches you enough art direction to ask for it again. AI replaces the designer, but first it briefly replaces design school.

Hartnup went on to make "a catalogue of one hundred poster styles, with ready-to-paste prompts and example images for each one." This solves the identikit-poster problem until everyone starts pasting the same risograph prompt, at which point every spring fayre will look like the same tastefully misregistered independent art magazine. The technology offers infinite aesthetic variety; humans will use it to converge on whichever five styles are in the prompt catalogue.

Two brains

Good news, perhaps, about why your brain does not always agree with itself:

For centuries, scientists have thought of the brain as a single, unified organ. But new research led by Stanford Medicine reveals that what we call the brain is two distinct organs that evolved independently over hundreds of millions of years.

One system became the forebrain and midbrain, responsible for language, consciousness, mathematics and wondering about its own origins. The other became the hindbrain, responsible for breathing, sleeping, heartbeat, hunger, swallowing and keeping the first system alive while it wonders about its origins.

The researchers found that the two regions arise from separate progenitor cells whose populations "never overlap." Similar two-origin patterns appear in chickens, zebrafish and acorn worms, suggesting that evolution took two ancient neural systems and pushed them together. "Having the brain as one organ would probably be more efficient," said researcher Kyle Loh, but that is not the structure we got.

So the human brain is essentially a 550-million-year-old merger whose systems were never fully integrated. One division writes poetry and does abstract reasoning; the other handles respiration and lunch. The merger is considered successful because they now "almost operate as one," which is honestly better integration than many corporate acquisitions achieve.

By identifying that the forebrain and hindbrain emerge from separate developmental pathways with unique genetic origins, the team resolved a long-standing challenge in stem cell biology that previously prevented the successful laboratory cultivation of hindbrain neurons. Consequently, this research offers a critical path forward for investigating the cellular mechanisms behind devastating neurodegenerative diseases like ALS and spinal muscular atrophy.
