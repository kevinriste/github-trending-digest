The HN Take

Beam

Reflection has announced Beam, a 501 billion-parameter open-weight model for coding, reasoning and agentic work. The weights are not open yet. They will be released later this month, after final red-teaming and evaluations. For now it is an open-weight model in the sense that there is a blog post saying that eventually you can have the weights.

Beam is a sparse mixture-of-experts model, so only 23 billion of its 501 billion parameters are active at a time. Reflection says this gives it "more intelligence per token": It approaches larger open models on coding and agentic tasks while using less inference compute. There is something pleasing about building 501 billion parameters and then emphasizing how few of them you need to use.

The training was less restrained. Reflection pretrained Beam on 23.8 trillion tokens, then used 10,500 Nvidia GB300 GPUs for four weeks to generate more than 100 million reinforcement-learning rollouts across roughly 1.3 billion sandboxes. At peak, the system supported 170,000 concurrent sandboxes. Seventy-one inference incidents occurred without ending the training job, and lost capacity amounted to just 0.02% of serving GPU-minutes. If you spend enough money on computers, even the failures become impressively efficient.

The reinforcement learning included a "controllable length penalty" that rewarded successful answers while discouraging unnecessary tokens. Early on, Beam got better while producing shorter answers. Later, as it developed more agentic capabilities, its answers became longer again, but Reflection says the extra tokens supported better performance. The model learned the essential intellectual progression from concise competence to having more to say about everything.

Also:

"When given web access, it organically learned to search for and query other large language models."

Sure. You pretrain a 501 billion-parameter model on 23.8 trillion tokens, run one of the largest reinforcement-learning programs conducted by an open lab, give the resulting intelligence access to the internet, and it discovers the highest form of reasoning: asking another model.

Dust

Backpropagation is the basic mechanism used to train modern neural networks. It calculates how each parameter contributed to an error and adjusts the parameters accordingly. It is mathematically structured, computationally efficient and so foundational that deep-learning hardware and architectures have evolved around it.

But what if you did not do that? What if you added random noise to the network, saw whether things got better, and repeated this with an enormous amount of compute?

That is roughly Dust, a new method for pretraining transformers without backpropagation. Dust perturbs activations independently at every token, measures how each perturbation changes the loss, and averages the results to estimate a gradient. Each token acts as a "virtual population member," allowing one forward pass to test thousands of perturbations in parallel.

The researchers call it "the first zeroth-order method that is competitive with backprop at pretraining transformer language models." At large populations, meaning substantially more compute, Dust sometimes exceeds backprop. They are admirably clear that they "do not attempt to make it compute-efficient enough to replace backprop today."

Still, the philosophical claim is interesting. Backpropagation might be a useful shortcut for a world where computation is scarce. In a world with sufficiently abundant computation, perhaps you can replace analytic structure with brute-force search. The paper also finds that larger models are often more population-efficient, not less, which is convenient: The proposed cure for needing too much compute is apparently to make the model bigger.

For now, calculus remains cheaper. But Dust offers a comforting vision of the future in which artificial intelligence no longer has to understand derivatives because it can afford to try everything.

5. Dust: Pretraining Transformers Without Backpropagation

qlabs.sh | E-Reverance | 206 points | 50 comments | discussion

First seen: October 06, 2026 | Consecutive daily streak: 1 day

Analysis

The paper introduces Dust, a zeroth-order training method that replaces backpropagation by adding independent Gaussian perturbations to transformer activations at each token, using resulting loss changes to estimate credit assignment and weight updates. By treating tokens as a “virtual population,” Dust evaluates many perturbation candidates in parallel within forward passes, making it far more efficient than weight-space evolutionary strategies such as EGGROLL. In small-scale language-model pretraining experiments, Dust approaches or occasionally surpasses backpropagation as the perturbation population grows, with larger models often benefiting more from additional population size and its gradient estimates increasingly aligning with backpropagation. The result is important because it suggests search-based, derivative-free training may be viable for transformers, but Dust currently requires substantially more computation than backpropagation and its strongest claims rely partly on extrapolations from relatively limited experiments.

Comment Analysis

The discussion was largely skeptical that Dust is a practical general replacement for backpropagation, with commenters emphasizing the value of gradient information and the current efficiency gap. More hopeful replies identified possible specialized settings, hybrid uses, and memory or scaling properties worth investigating. A recurring disagreement was whether reduced coordination could yield an advantage on real hardware, where coordination may already be inexpensive.

Backprop has a fundamental advantage — These commenters argued that gradient information offers useful direction on typical neural-network objectives, making zeroth-order search inefficient by comparison. Some also pointed to gradient-like alternatives for less conventional objectives.

The gradient is useful. Instead of trying random directions and hoping that one of them is an improvement, it tells you where to go.blt
First-order methods don't explore the loss landscape optimally, but the loss function tends to be nonconvex, and zeroth-order methods don't address that issue head on.syntacticsalt

Zeroth-order methods may have specialized uses — Some commenters saw potential where gradient-like information is unavailable or where a particular device or training setup could favor zeroth-order methods. These comments described possible niches rather than a general replacement for backpropagation.
