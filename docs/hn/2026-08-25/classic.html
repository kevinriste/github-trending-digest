The HN Take

Tipless.

Here is a strange restaurant innovation:

"What you see is what you pay."

La Cigale in San Francisco charges $140 per person, pays wine waiter Caroline Kraetzer $40 an hour and does not accept tips. Nightshade Noodle Bar in Massachusetts has also eliminated tipping, in part because kitchen workers were taking home "a fraction of what the front staff made" and because tipping let customers pay workers differently based on gender, race or sexuality.

The basic idea is that the restaurant calculates how much it costs to employ people, puts that cost into the menu price and pays the employees. This is generally how businesses work. If you buy a laptop, you do not pay the listed price and then have a touchscreen suggest that you give the assembly worker another 20%.

Restaurants are different. The traditional model is that the restaurant sells you dinner at one price, and then at the end it asks you to independently determine the compensation of one particular employee based on your mood, arithmetic skills and feelings about the employee. This system has some disadvantages. The employee's income is unpredictable. Kitchen staff do not participate much. Customers might discriminate. Also the employee spends time opening and closing the restaurant when there are no customers around to compensate them.

The alternative is to put the full labor cost in the listed price. This has the disadvantage that customers can see it.

"Higher menu prices make dining out seem more expensive because people do not adequately take into account that they are no longer tipping," says Cornell professor William Michael Lynn. "It leads to lower demand."

Right. A $120 dinner that costs $120 looks more expensive than a $100 dinner that costs $120. The second restaurant has lower prices because 20% of the price is revealed only after you have eaten.

There are also actual tax consequences. Talulla in Cambridge raised menu prices 23% when it eliminated tips, but eventually switched back. Tips do not count as restaurant revenue, while higher menu prices do, so replacing tips with prices can increase sales tax. The economic substance might be similar, but the formal structure is different: In one model the restaurant pays its staff, while in the other the customer apparently makes a series of spontaneous gifts to strangers. The tax system respects the romance.

Local Paint.

"Generated locally" is a technical term meaning that Microsoft sends your prompt to a server, gets back a unique identifier, runs the image model on your computer and invisibly embeds the server-issued identifier into the resulting pixels.

A reverse engineer looked at the AI features in Microsoft Paint and Photos and found that the apps ship local image models. On supported Copilot+ PCs, the image generation itself can run locally. But prompt moderation remains remote:

"Microsoft receives and moderates the prompt, then issues the unique GUID that Paint embeds into the locally generated image."

The GUID is inserted using a separate invisible-watermark function. Turning off Paint's visible Copilot watermark does not turn off the invisible one. If the invisible watermarking fails, Paint fails the entire generation rather than returning an unmarked image.

This is all arguably sensible provenance engineering. Microsoft also adds signed C2PA metadata identifying the content as AI-generated, and the identifier in that metadata matches the identifier embedded in the pixels. If someone strips the file metadata, the invisible watermark can still connect the image to its provenance record.

Still, "local" is doing fairly narrow work here. The matrix multiplication happens in your house. The prompt goes to Microsoft, Microsoft assigns the generation a serial number, and Paint modifies the image so that the serial number travels with it. Paint also sends the previous prompt-generation identifier with the next moderation request, allowing successive requests to be linked. Local generation means that Microsoft does not have to generate the picture, not that Microsoft does not participate in the generation.

There is even a file-format consequence. Paint restricts direct saves of generated images to formats that preserve C2PA metadata, including PNG, JPEG, GIF and its own .paint format. BMP, the classic Paint format, is absent because it cannot embed the manifest in the same way.

Microsoft Paint can now generate an image locally, but it cannot simply paint it.

Analysis

A growing number of US restaurants are abandoning the traditional tipping model in favor of higher menu prices to provide staff with consistent, equitable wages. By eliminating gratuities, owners aim to reduce pay disparities between front-of-house and kitchen employees while insulating staff from the volatility of customer generosity and systemic bias. However, this transition remains a significant business challenge, as higher menu prices often create the perception of increased costs, which can dampen demand and complicate tax obligations. Ultimately, while the model offers greater financial stability for workers and helps combat industry-wide "tipping fatigue," the economic disadvantages—including potential revenue drops and difficulty retaining staff accustomed to high tip earnings—suggest that widespread adoption remains unlikely in the near future.

4. MS Paint and Photos inivisibly watermark even locally generated output with GUID

xusheng.dev | ComputerGuru | 730 points | 328 comments | discussion

First seen: August 25, 2026 | Consecutive daily streak: 1 day

Analysis

Recent security research has revealed that Microsoft Paint and Photos embed invisible, server-issued GUID watermarks into images generated via AI, even when the actual image synthesis occurs locally on the user's device. These applications require an internet connection to send prompts to Microsoft servers for moderation, where a unique identifier is generated and subsequently embedded into the pixels of the resulting image using a content-adaptive, SVD-style algorithm. This invisible watermark is cryptographically linked to the file's C2PA metadata, creating a multi-layered provenance system that acts as a "soft binding" to verify the image's AI origin. The discovery is significant because it highlights that "local" AI features often rely on silent, remote infrastructure for monitoring and tracking, raising questions regarding user privacy and the extent of transparency in how AI-generated content is labeled and traced.

The discussion was dominated by concern that an undisclosed, persistent identifier and remote moderation undermine the privacy expected from local image generation.
