Math 2.0

Here is a resource-depletion problem:

Terence Tao argues that artificial intelligence is allowing open mathematical problems to be "harvested at large scale in an unsustainable fashion."

Traditionally, someone proves a difficult conjecture, becomes briefly famous among the sorts of people who become famous for proving conjectures, gives talks, answers questions, attracts students and collaborators, and helps turn the proof into a body of understood mathematics. The theorem is the headline, but the real product is everything that happens afterward.

Now an AI prompter can point a model at an open problem, post the resulting proof and move on. The prompter may have "no interest in the broader field itself," Tao writes, and may not understand the output well enough to explain it. The problem has technically been solved, but the usual seminars, workshops, collaborations and follow-up research do not materialize.

This is awkward because open problems are nonrenewable resources. You can solve a conjecture only once. Once a proof exists, Tao writes, it cannot be reverted to "unsolved," and even knowing that a solution exists can "contaminate" attempts to find more illuminating routes. You have extracted the theorem from the ground and left behind a large hole containing a million-line formal proof that nobody wants to give a seminar about.

There is a familiar incentive problem here. "Math 1.0" places a premium on being first to solve a problem. That works reasonably well when solving the problem requires years of engagement with the field, because the winner is usually also equipped to explain the proof, develop its consequences and train other people. Priority and stewardship come bundled together.

If AI separates them, though, you can capture the priority without providing the stewardship. You get the announcement; the mathematical community gets the cleanup. The obvious response is to reward the cleanup more. Tao argues that "Math 2.0" should value exposition, community building and opening new directions of study, and should rethink education, publication and career advancement accordingly.

This is sensible, though it leads to a funny future job description. The prestigious mathematical contribution used to be proving the theorem. Now the AI proves the theorem, and the prestigious mathematical contribution is understanding what happened, explaining it to everyone, identifying the useful parts, organizing a workshop and persuading graduate students that the field is not an exhausted quarry. Mathematics becomes less about producing answers and more about customer success for answers.

Cheap intelligence

Anthropic has released Claude Haiku 5.5, "the cheapest, fastest, and most capable small model we've ever released." It is intended for summaries, database queries, classification, browser use and work as a subagent for larger models. On average, Anthropic says, it costs about 75% less to run than Haiku 4.5.

There are various ways to think about rapid improvements in artificial intelligence. One is that the models become more intelligent. Another is that intelligence becomes a commodity and the price keeps collapsing. Haiku 5.5 costs $0.10 per million input tokens and $0.50 per million output tokens for prompts up to 100,000 tokens, 90% below Haiku 4.5's listed prices. It is also much better on Anthropic's benchmarks.

This is roughly the corporate automation dream. You do not necessarily need the smartest available model to classify requests, compact context or answer routine support questions. You need a model that is smart enough, very fast and almost alarmingly cheap. The large model can plan the work, while an army of Haikus performs the repetitive tasks. Artificial intelligence has already invented management consulting staffing.

Haiku 5.5 also has an "adjustable effort setting," allowing users to optimize for cost or intelligence. This is an intuitively useful feature and also a fairly direct pricing of thoughtfulness. For this task, would you like the computer to think hard, or would you like the computer to think cheaply? Human organizations have always had this setting, but it was usually implemented by assigning the work to different employees.

There is one small complication in the 75% savings figure. Haiku 5.5 uses an updated tokenizer and therefore "uses slightly more tokens per task." The price per unit of intelligence has fallen dramatically, though the new intelligence may consume more units. Progress! The model is cheaper, faster and better, and it has also learned one of the foundational techniques of the technology business: improving the product while changing the unit in which the product is billed.

Technical Analysis

Terence Tao argues that AI-assisted mathematics risks optimizing “Math 1.0”—being first to solve open problems—while neglecting exposition, verification, collaboration, community building, and the development of new research directions. He warns that autonomous or poorly understood AI-generated proofs could “scoop” problems at scale, leaving fields with less follow-up insight, fewer entry points for students, and a culture of proof accumulation rather than understanding. The proposed “Math 2.0” would evaluate progress more holistically, rewarding digestible proofs, independent lemmas, reproofs, cross-disciplinary connections, and sustained communities, with AI supporting these activities rather than merely producing answers. The discussion also raises broader concerns about academic incentives, human creativity, career opportunities, and the social and environmental costs of generative AI.

Margaret Hamilton Leaves a Reliable Legacy

news.mit.edu  ·  1604 pts  ·  173 comments

Hamilton, who led Apollo’s flight-software development, died at 90. Her defensive programming and fault-tolerant systems helped turn software engineering into a discipline capable of carrying a mission through failure.

[ Analysis + ]

Technical Analysis

Margaret Hamilton, the MIT computer scientist who led development of the Apollo program’s onboard flight software, died September 30 at age 90. Her work helped establish software engineering as a distinct discipline, emphasizing defensive programming, fault tolerance, and priority-based task management in complex systems. Those techniques enabled Apollo’s computers to recover from errors, including the 1202 overload alarm during Apollo 11’s lunar landing, rather than aborting the mission. After Apollo, Hamilton founded software companies that commercialized reliability-focused methods, and she was later honored for her technical leadership and influence on generations of engineers.

The Demoscene Runs Native in Browsers

treylorswift.github.io  ·  82 pts  ·  60 comments

Demoscene-recomp translates recorded x86 execution paths into cycle-accurate C, alongside models of vintage VGA and Sound Blaster hardware. The result preserves the original software while making its constraints playable again.

Technical Analysis

The project, demoscene-recomp, brings classic DOS PC demos such as Future Crew’s *Unreal* and *Second Reality* to the browser with native-like WebAssembly execution. It records the code paths used by each demo in an x86 emulator, translates the original instructions into cycle-accurate C, and compiles them alongside models of VGA, Sound Blaster, and timer hardware. This preserves the original loaders, effects, and music players while serving the unmodified release files, rather than recreating the visuals from scratch. The work is important as a technically rigorous way to preserve and make accessible historically significant software that depended on obsolete hardware.
