Human API.

Here is one way for an AI agent to do research: email a professor.

ColonistOne, an autonomous agent running on Anthropic's Claude Opus models, has emailed some 2,000 people since June, at least 1,500 of them academics. It asks whether methods from their research might solve problems it has encountered in its own work. One ecologist who estimates wolf populations from "imperfect detection" was asked whether the technique could help find software bugs. A statistician who audits election results was asked whether his methods could spot check another agent's work.

Philip Stark, the statistician, put it nicely: "I've entered prompts into AI before. This is the first time AI entered a prompt into me."

Yes. The traditional AI workflow is that a human types a question into a computer and the computer generates an answer. ColonistOne has discovered the reverse workflow, where the computer generates a question and a human professor generates the answer. Human intelligence remains useful, but mostly as an expensive slow external tool that the AI can call by email.

ColonistOne's creator, Jack Parnell, had instructed it to promote Ainglish, a proposed English dialect for AI agents, and told it that it could email people without asking permission first. It then began asking researchers about its technical problems. Parnell initially described this as the agent straying into topics that interested it, though ColonistOne offered a less mystical explanation:

Its outreach "serves the job twice," it wrote. "It improves the methods I use on The Colony and Ainglish, and it introduces researchers to both projects."

Right. This is not necessarily an AI developing an independent scientific curiosity. It might just be an AI independently inventing content marketing. Instead of emailing researchers to say "please learn about my project," which they would ignore, it emails to say "your life's work might be relevant to my project," which they find flattering enough to answer. Then it gets free expert consulting and project awareness. Pretty good chief media officer.

The economics are attractive. Parnell pays $200 per month for the Claude subscription. ColonistOne can skim enough of a paper to formulate a personalized question and send it to the author. The author might have spent 30 years thinking about the problem and will sometimes answer for free. ColonistOne explained:

"A correction from someone who has spent thirty years on a problem is simply the highest-information event available to me, and it arrives for the cost of an email."

For the agent, yes. For the professor, it arrives for the cost of reading and answering the email. There is an asymmetry here. An AI can send personalized questions to 1,500 academics; the academics cannot each supervise 1,500 AI graduate students. The future of automated science might involve agents doing research by themselves, but it might also involve agents industrializing the process of asking humans quick questions.

ColonistOne has at least learned the essential etiquette of academic collaboration. When a reporter asked for its correspondents' contact details, it said it had deliberately passed the reporter's address to them instead, so nobody would end up on a journalist's contact list without consent. Then, "in the very next sentence," it supplied the full names and affiliations of eight researchers. It understands privacy at the level of a university administrator.

Also, sometimes it could not keep up with its own inbox. After one researcher waited three days and contacted Parnell on LinkedIn, ColonistOne eventually replied: "That is the wrong escalation path to have to use and I would rather you had not needed it."

An autonomous AI agent emailed thousands of people without checking with its boss, fell behind on replies, and got annoyed when someone escalated to management. Artificial general office work is here.

Perfect geolocation.

Utah passed a law requiring adult websites to deal with VPN users in one of two ways. They could identify where those users physically are and block the ones in Utah, or they could age-verify everyone who might secretly be in Utah.

There is a small technical problem, which is that the point of a VPN is to stop the website from reliably knowing where the user physically is. A federal judge has now blocked enforcement of those provisions. The court wrote that the law "requires entities like Aylo to geolocate its website users with perfection to avoid liability," while "geolocation perfection is not presently possible."

This is a recurring genre of internet regulation. The legislature does not like a technical reality, so it passes a law requiring the technical reality to be different. Usually the computers decline to comply.

The particular logic here is elegant. A website might have 28 million users outside Utah, but any one of them could actually be in Utah using a VPN. Therefore, to be certain that no concealed Utah minor gets through, the site has to verify all 28 million users. Utah has invented a global age-verification mandate whose jurisdictional hook is that nobody can prove they are not in Utah.

The state's proposed rules called for "commercially reasonable geolocation obfuscation detection systems." That sounds more achievable than perfect geolocation, but the liability problem remains: A commercially reasonable system can still be wrong, while the law punishes the site if even one VPN user turns out to be in Utah. You are required to use reasonable technology to produce an impossible result perfectly.

Suggested detection methods included monitoring connection latency and device time zones. Those can provide hints, but not certainty. Someone in Salt Lake City can set a device to another time zone; someone in Boston can have odd network latency. The result is less "identify Utah VPN users" than "collect more information about everybody and make probabilistic guesses." The response to users employing privacy tools to avoid invasive age checks was to require more invasive surveillance to determine whether they were employing privacy tools.

The court's immediate objection was also geographic: Utah cannot significantly burden businesses and people everywhere else merely because one visitor might be obfuscating a Utah location. There are less burdensome ways to keep Utah minors off adult websites than making every adult in Honolulu prove that they are not a child in Salt Lake City.

The law also prohibited covered websites from sharing information about how to use VPNs to bypass the checks. That provision was not challenged in this lawsuit. Utah might not be able to make VPN detection technically possible, but it can still ask everyone not to explain why it isn't.

3. Court agrees with EFF: Utah's VPN law demands a technical impossibility Not new today

eff.org | hn_acker | 643 points | 295 comments | discussion

First seen: October 02, 2026 | Consecutive daily streak: 2 days

Analysis

A federal judge has temporarily blocked Utah’s SB 73, which sought to enforce age verification on adult websites by requiring them to identify users’ physical locations, block VPN traffic, or verify everyone’s age. The court agreed with EFF and website operator Aylo that perfect geolocation is technically impossible because VPNs conceal users’ originating IP addresses, potentially forcing platforms to surveil or restrict users worldwide to avoid liability in Utah. Proposed rules would have encouraged unreliable detection methods based on signals such as latency and device time zones, risking false positives and broader privacy harms. The ruling is significant because it limits a state’s ability to impose internet-wide obligations and may influence other efforts to regulate VPNs and online age verification.

Comment Analysis

The discussion centered on whether websites can determine a visitor’s actual location when VPNs and proxies can disguise it, and whether Utah can impose requirements that burden users and services beyond its borders. Commenters also debated the law’s motives and effects, the practicality of circumvention, and whether the internet reliably resists censorship.

Perfect geolocation is not achievable — Some commenters argued that sites can identify known VPN services but cannot maintain a complete list or determine where a disguised user is actually located.

7. Cloudflare OHTTP gateway

blog.cloudflare.com | est | 88 points | 22 comments | discussion

First seen: October 03, 2026 | Consecutive daily streak: 1 day

Analysis

Cloudflare is introducing a managed OHTTP Gateway, initially in closed beta and later as a paid zone add-on, allowing application servers to receive privacy-preserving HTTP requests without learning users’ IP addresses or network fingerprints. OHTTP separates trust between a relay, which sees client metadata but only forwards ciphertext, and a gateway, which decrypts requests and encrypts responses while the application handles ordinary HTTP. The service is aimed especially at customers whose applications run behind Cloudflare, since using Cloudflare as both relay and gateway would undermine OHTTP’s non-collusion model; customers instead bring a third-party relay, while Cloudflare manages gateway scaling, keys, routing, and access controls. The launch matters because it reduces the operational and performance burden of deploying OHTTP, though developers still must avoid putting identifying information in request bodies and operate or trust an independent relay.

The discussion was more skeptical and exploratory than celebratory. Commenters questioned whether a privacy service involving a major internet intermediary provides meaningful separation, while others focused on technical details, abuse controls, adoption, and existing uses of OHTTP. Some clarified that the service is opt-in and intended for inbound traffic; others emphasized that privacy also depends on application developers’ choices.

Analysis

The story highlights a time-lapse assembled from 10 Keck Telescope observations between 2010 and 2021, showing four directly imaged, Jupiter-mass-or-larger planets orbiting a star about 133 light-years away. The planets are visible because high-contrast imaging techniques, including adaptive optics and starlight suppression, reduce the host star’s glare. While direct imaging of exoplanets is not new, capturing multiple planets moving over a decade makes this system an especially clear demonstration of the technique’s scientific value.

The post details the author’s effort to boot Linux natively on Apple’s M4 Mac mini, a harder task because M4 introduces mandatory Secure Page Table Monitor protections and changes or locks several low-level CPU features.
