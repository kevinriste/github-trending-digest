The HN Take

Infinite scroll

Here is a data visualization with a fairly literal user interface:

"The graph is U.S. household wealth drawn to scale, where one pixel is $5,000 and the top 1% is so far up you have to scroll for minutes to reach it."

The median US household has net worth of $211,292. The 99th percentile starts at $15,033,455. The average top-1% household has $40,434,990, and the average top-0.1% household has $184,209,560. At one pixel per $5,000, the climb to that last number is 36,842 pixels.

This is intuitive. Normally a chart of wealth inequality fits on one screen because the chart designer uses a logarithmic scale, truncates the axis or otherwise applies the modern graphical principle that websites should not be 36,842 pixels tall. But those choices make the distribution look less weird. Here the point is that the distribution is weird, so the chart makes you experience it physically, by scrolling upward for a very long time while increasingly annoyed with rich people and your mouse wheel.

There is also a printable PDF that you can tape together on a wall. "We dare you to print the whole thing," says the site. The average top-1% household's wealth, stacked in $100 bills, would be almost 145 feet tall. Represented in ordinary printer paper, it will mostly be a monument to your toner budget.

The chart also illustrates a useful statistical distinction. The 99th percentile is about $15 million, but the average household within the top 1% has about $40 million. You cross the line into the 1% and think, great, you've arrived. Then you discover that the average member is almost three times richer than the minimum qualifying member, and the average member of the top 0.1% is more than four times richer than that. Wealth inequality is fractal. At every level, you can zoom in and find a smaller group making the previous group look poor.

Meanwhile the poorest 7% of households owe more than they own, and the poorest 8% have net worth of $1 or less. Their section of the chart is quite compact. Negative wealth is extremely efficient on screen.

Git blame Congress

A programmer can click a line of code and find out who wrote it, when it changed and what it looked like before. A lawyer reading federal law often gets amendment notes written in prose.

This seems backward. Software is allowed to contain bugs; law is what determines whether you go to prison. Yet the software has a clean commit history, while the law is like "please consult the footnotes and several later statutes, one of which retroactively changed what this paragraph meant during a three-month period four years ago."

Lexifina is building, approximately, Git for the US Code. This is harder than putting the Code in a repository and saving each new version. For one thing, outside titles enacted as positive law, the Code is only "prima facie evidence of the law"; if it conflicts with the Statutes at Large, the Statutes at Large win. For another, the date Congress passes an amendment and the date the amendment takes effect can differ. Sometimes a later law reaches backward and changes the legal answer for an earlier date.

The example is the definition of a small business debtor in bankruptcy law. Congress temporarily raised the debt limit to $7.5 million in 2020, extended it, let it lapse in March 2022, and restored it in June 2022 with language reaching back over the gap. If you ask what the law said for a case filed in May 2022, the Code published in May gives one answer and a law passed in June changes it. Legal time travel, but with jurisdictional consequences.

This is a particular problem for language models. A model can apply an obsolete statute because that is what appeared in its training data, or apply the newest statute to facts from a time when the older one governed. Asking it to reconstruct the old law is not enough: Research cited by Lexifina found generated versions that looked 93% to 99% like the official text but often contained legally significant errors. Ninety-nine percent textual similarity is excellent for summarizing a restaurant review and less reassuring when the missing 1% is "not."

So Lexifina took pairs of official release points from 2017 onward, applied the intervening public laws in date order and compared the reconstructed text with the next official version. Across nine titles, 361 of 365 changes matched exactly. The other four are labeled according to how far they could be confirmed. Each change records the law that made it and when it took effect.

There are still limitations. The system does not yet display later laws that reach backward, so the 2022 bankruptcy gap can still produce the historically published answer rather than the answer Congress retroactively imposed. But this is the right basic idea: statutory text should have versions, provenance and dates as first-class data.

Eventually lawyers will be able to run git blame on a sentence and discover that Congress wrote it. This will not resolve the blame question, exactly, but it will improve the tooling.

3. Git for the US Code

lexifina.com | alansaber | 283 points | 139 comments | discussion

First seen: October 28, 2026 | Consecutive daily streak: 1 day

Analysis

Lexifina presents a “Git for the US Code,” a system that reconstructs federal statutes across successive release points, attributes each change to its public law, and records when it took effect. Its date-aware interface lets users view the text in force on a particular date, inspect changed language, and trace amendments and related definitions—capabilities that are difficult to obtain from the Code’s prose amendment and effective-date notes. The project successfully reproduced 361 of 365 tested changes across nine titles, but currently has limitations around retroactive amendments and unusual effective-date rules. This provenance matters for lawyers and legal AI systems because statutes can change, expire, or apply retroactively, and models that ignore temporal validity may produce plausible but legally incorrect answers.

Comment Analysis

Commenters welcomed the prospect of versioned, date-aware law, while differing over whether it would improve research, accountability, or public understanding. They also debated whether Git fits legal texts, whether attribution and transparency have costs, and how the project relates to existing systems and public access.
