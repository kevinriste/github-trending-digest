European orbit

Europe has a rocket company now:

Isar Aerospace said its Spectrum rocket reached orbit and deployed satellites on Saturday, making the German company "the first commercial space company from Europe to successfully deliver satellites into orbit," on only its second flight.

"Europe now has sovereign access to space," said Chief Executive Officer Daniel Metzler.

Sure. Sovereignty is partly a political concept, partly an engineering concept and partly a manufacturing-throughput concept. You are not meaningfully sovereign in space if you can build a satellite but have to ask someone else to put it there. Isar launched from its own complex in Norway, crossed the Karman line, completed stage separation and a circularization burn, and deployed the payloads. It is still working with customers to confirm the satellites' status, so Europe has sovereign access to space and is now checking whether space has sovereign access to the satellites.

The next problem is scale. Isar says launch remains "the largest bottleneck for the global space industry," and it is building what it calls the "machine behind the machine": a vertically integrated, highly automated production system for rockets. Spectrum vehicles 3 through 7 are already in production, and a new 40,000-square-meter facility is intended eventually to produce as many as 40 launch vehicles a year.

The first machine goes to space. The second machine makes the first machine. If that works, eventually Europe's main space achievement will be having a rocket factory with a backlog, which is less romantic than planting a flag on the moon but considerably more useful.

Copyleft by token

Here is one response to artificial intelligence:

Software developer Henri Bergius has changed his default license from the permissive MIT license to the European Union Public License 1.2, a strong copyleft license that requires reciprocal licensing even when software is offered as a service.

His diagnosis is blunt:

"We won the debate, and gained little for users or developers. All that our efforts did was to make it easier for big corporations build things more cheaply and for billionaires to become trillionaires."

The classic open-source bargain was that developers gave away code, companies used it, and perhaps everyone benefited from the resulting ecosystem. Bergius's view is that the companies benefited rather more concretely. So now they can comply with the copyleft terms or "spend the effort or tokens to build their own."

That last bit is the modern wrinkle. Strong copyleft says: You cannot take my work, improve it privately and sell access without sharing the result. AI says: Fine, describe the work to a model and ask it to produce something similar from scratch. The developer's negotiating position has advanced from "you may use my code for free" to "you may obey my terms or burn some tokens recreating it." This is progress, though perhaps mostly for the token vendors.

The EUPL does have legally valid official translations in 23 languages, which is useful. Corporations can now understand that they are unwelcome with unusual precision across most of Europe.

bergie.iki.fi  ·  12 pts  ·  3 comments

Technical Analysis

After decades of using permissive licenses like MIT and LGPL, software developer Henri Bergius has transitioned his personal projects to the European Union Public License (EUPL) 1.2. He argues that permissive licensing has primarily benefited large corporations at the expense of individual developers, leading him to adopt a strong copyleft approach that closes the "SaaS loophole" to ensure reciprocal sharing. This shift highlights a growing disillusionment among some open-source contributors regarding the current ecosystem's balance of power. By choosing the EUPL, Bergius also benefits from the license's official legal translations across 23 languages, signaling a deliberate move toward international accessibility over Valley-centric norms.

Technical Analysis

Bryan Cantrill argues that the increasing prevalence of LLM-generated content is alienating readers, who quickly identify and reject "AI-slop" that lacks human authenticity. Technical Analysis

Analysis

Benedict Evans argues that while AI offers powerful new capabilities for automation, it will not simply replace existing software architectures or turn every employee into an effective tool-builder. Companies operate through a complex, institutionalized spectrum of systems, and most meaningful transformation requires identifying non-obvious problems rather than merely simplifying code generation. The author notes that widespread deployment of AI assistants currently mirrors the adoption patterns of past technologies, where broad access yields marginal gains while structural change remains a difficult, slow process of organizational redesign. Ultimately, the story is important because it highlights that real technological transformation occurs not just through faster execution of existing tasks, but through the eventual emergence of entirely new business models that were previously impossible.
