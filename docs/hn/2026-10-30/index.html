Le Chonk

Mistral has released the weights of Mistral Large 4, its 1-trillion-parameter multimodal model, which it calls "Le Chonk." This is perhaps the correct naming convention for trillion-parameter models. You can discuss mixture-of-experts routing and token efficiency if you want, but eventually you have built an enormous French computer and named it Le Chonk.

Mistral says the model is now the best open-weight model for coding. On Cognition's private FrontierCode 2.0 benchmark, which measures whether a repository maintainer would actually merge an agent's change, it scored 19.7%.

This is leading performance. Four times out of five, the best open-weight coding model submits a change that the maintainer would not merge. To be fair, this is also a reasonable description of human software development.

The more important claim is efficiency. Mistral Large 4 tied the leading open-weight model on the Artificial Analysis Coding Agent Index while using 47% fewer tokens per task, and improved by 13 percentage points on Terminal-Bench while cutting output tokens by 30%. There are three reasoning modes: Low, Medium and High. Low is for simple tasks, Medium is for everyday tasks and High is for difficult tasks requiring precision. The model defaults to High, because no one wants to admit that their task is Medium.

Mistral is also emphasizing cybersecurity. The model handles defensive work such as reproducing vulnerabilities and writing patches, while refusing 96.6% of malicious cyber requests, "the highest we measure among open models." But:

"Though we observed the model refusing to comply with black-box attacks against live targets, we see a potential for uplift to conduct real attacks. We are modifying our guardrails accordingly, and will continue updating them in the months to come."

Sure. Mistral is releasing the model weights so that organizations can "inspect, customize, and deploy" the model without depending on Mistral. It is also continuing to update the guardrails. These ideas coexist somewhat awkwardly. If customers truly own and control the model, then Mistral can update the official guardrails, but customers can control those too. Sovereign AI means that you do not have to ask a foreign company for permission to run your artificial intelligence. It also means that your artificial intelligence does not have to ask the foreign company's guardrails for permission to hack something.

The normal commercial pitch for closed AI is: Our model is powerful, and we will operate it safely for you. The pitch for open-weight AI is: Our model is powerful, and now safety is your problem. Sovereignty!

Black hole photography

Here is how you take a picture of a black hole:

You don't, exactly. Nothing, including light, escapes from a black hole, which is inconvenient for photography. Instead you photograph the bright material around it and infer the dark region in the middle. This is called photographing the black hole's shadow, which is fair enough. Most photography is capturing light reflected from things; black-hole photography is capturing the conspicuous absence of light from a thing that eats light.

The other difficulty is that the black hole is very far away and appears very small. Shep Doeleman and the Event Horizon Telescope team solved this by linking radio telescopes around the world, effectively turning Earth into one telescope. A team of 250 researchers used the planet as a camera to photograph something that cannot be seen.

The result was the first image of the supermassive black hole in the galaxy M87, about 55 million light-years away, followed by an image of the black hole at the center of our galaxy. Movies may be possible by the early 2030s.

This is the standard progression of media technology. First humanity spends decades building a planet-sized instrument to obtain one blurry still image. Then someone asks when it will support video.

Earth Becomes a Camera for Black Holes

quantamagazine.org  ·  11 pts  ·  5 comments

A planet-spanning network of radio telescopes resolves the shadow of a black hole. Atomic clocks, demanding frequencies, and decades of coordination turn an invisible boundary into an image—and eventually, perhaps, a moving picture.

Read →Share →
[ Analysis + ]

Technical Analysis

The story, presented as an interview with astrophysicist Shep Doeleman, explains how the Event Horizon Telescope captured images of black-hole shadows by combining radio telescopes worldwide into an Earth-sized instrument. Using very-long-baseline interferometry, hydrogen maser clocks, high-frequency observations, and extensive data processing, researchers achieved the resolution needed to observe the ring of light predicted by general relativity. The effort required decades of technical development and coordination among existing observatories and roughly 250 researchers. These images provide direct evidence of supermassive black holes and let scientists test predictions about gravity, accretion, and the role black holes play in galaxies, with future time-resolved movies potentially possible.

Technical Analysis
