The HN Take

Cruise control

Here is a useful scale comparison:

Carnival Corporation, the world's largest luxury cruise operator, emitted nearly 10 times more sulphur oxide around European coasts than did all 260 million European cars in 2017, according to an analysis by Transport & Environment.

Royal Caribbean, the second-largest cruise operator, emitted four times as much as Europe's cars.

There is a boring explanation for this astonishing comparison, which is that Europe has regulated sulphur pollution from cars rather more strictly than sulphur pollution from cruise ships. Cruise ships can burn extremely sulphurous bunker fuel along much of the European coast, while cars cannot. If you make one industry stop emitting a particular pollutant and let another industry keep emitting it, the second industry will eventually look bad compared with 260 million members of the first industry.

Still, 10 times is 10 times. Cities have spent years restricting dirty diesel cars, while welcoming floating cities that burn "some of the dirtiest fuel possible." Barcelona, Palma de Mallorca and Venice were the most affected port cities in the analysis. In Marseille, 57 cruise ships emitted almost as much nitrogen oxide in 2017 as one-quarter of the city's 340,000 passenger cars.

The policy recommendation is straightforward: Require cleaner fuel, expand marine emission-control areas and make ships use shore-side electricity while in port. Batteries can cover shorter journeys, and the report argues that hydrogen could eventually power even the largest ships.

The basic tourism model is that you fly to a beautiful European city, get on a giant boat, burn dirty fuel beside the city, briefly get off to admire its historic architecture, and then complain that the city center has banned your taxi.

Frostbite

Okay here is an AI chip-design story:

Architect Labs says its AI systems autonomously designed a new general-purpose CPU architecture called Frostbite, iterating across "the compiler, ISA, architecture, microarchitecture, RTL, verification, and final GDS."

On 25 workloads, including 15 held out until final evaluation, Frostbite achieved 4.59 times the performance per watt of a modern silicon-proven dual-issue, in-order CPU core.

That sounds important! There are several qualifications. Frostbite has reached final GDS, not working silicon, and its power numbers are pre-silicon estimates. The comparison CPU is silicon-proven; Frostbite is formally verified and physically modeled. Also Frostbite is built around interaction nets, a programming model in which computation consists of local graph rewrites, dependencies are explicit and every value has exactly one owner. Programs are written in Vine and compiled into that representation. "General-purpose CPU" is doing some work there.

But the central idea is interesting. Conventional processors take programs whose dependencies have been obscured by shared mutable state, and then spend power and silicon rediscovering which instructions can run in parallel. Frostbite starts with a representation where the dependencies were never hidden. It can therefore avoid some of the machinery used to recover parallelism and maintain coherent caches. If software tells the hardware exactly what depends on what, the hardware can stop being such an expensive detective.

The AI-design process is perhaps as interesting as the processor. Architect Labs built a formal specification language called ASL, which served as the machine-checkable source of truth across system models, RTL, verification and theorem proving. The final specification lowered into more than 800,000 lines of Lean. Agent swarms proposed architectural changes in parallel, checked them against the contracts, implemented them, modeled their power use and kept 37 of 58 ideas.

This is a sensible response to the standard problem with having AI design hardware, which is that hardware hallucinations are expensive. If an AI hallucinates in a chatbot, it tells you that glue is a pizza topping. If it hallucinates in an ASIC, you manufacture a large quantity of decorative silicon. So you give it enormous freedom to explore, but surround that freedom with formal proofs, frozen contracts and verification.

Architect Labs says Frostbite was designed with "zero architectural priors." It also chose the workloads, programming model, formal guardrails and evaluation process, so this does not mean that an AI woke up one morning, contemplated the transistor and independently invented a CPU. It means that humans built a tightly constrained scientific environment in which AI agents could search a large design space much faster than humans normally would.

Which is still the maximalist AI automation story, only in a more credible order. First the AI replaces the people who design the chips. Then the chips run better AI. Then the better AI designs better chips. Eventually the only remaining human job is choosing the benchmark suite, which is fortunate because whoever chooses the benchmark suite tends to win.

2. Frostbite: A new CPU architecture discovered, designed and verified by AI

architectlabs.com | shioyama | 137 points | 93 comments | discussion

First seen: November 20, 2026 | Consecutive daily streak: 1 day

Analysis

Architect Labs describes Frostbite, a general-purpose CPU architecture and instruction set autonomously co-designed and formally verified by AI around interaction nets, which expose explicit dependencies, local computation, and single-use values. Its AI agents jointly optimized the compiler, runtime, ISA, microarchitecture, RTL, verification, and physical layout using a machine-checkable specification and rapid software modeling. In pre-silicon 7 nm evaluations, one Frostbite quad reportedly achieved 4.59× the performance per watt and 8.77× the performance per area of a comparable dual-issue in-order core across 25 workloads, though results were strongest on parallel tasks and have not yet been validated in silicon or against broader modern CPUs. The project matters as a demonstration of AI-assisted hardware/software co-design and a possible alternative to cache-heavy conventional CPUs, with future work including FPGA access, an operating system, broader benchmarks, and releasing the RTL and ISA.

First seen: November 20, 2026 | Consecutive daily streak: 1 day

Analysis

An OpenSSL pull request addresses CVE-2026-121481, a critical buffer overflow in `EVP_EncodeBlock()` caused by invalid inputs such as null buffers, negative lengths, or encoded sizes exceeding the range of an `int`. The proposed fix returns `-1` for these cases, adds similar validation to `EVP_DecodeBlock()`, corrects a related length check in `http_client.c`, and includes tests and documentation. The flaw may be exploitable through `openssl s_client` by supplying an exceptionally large proxy password, though the official advisory remains pending. The issue is important because it affects a widely used cryptographic library and resembles a previous OpenSSL encoding vulnerability.
