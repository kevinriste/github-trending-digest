Penny universities

Here is a theory of the Scientific Revolution: coffee.

A new paper by Monir Bounadi and Jens Oehlen studies the spread of coffeehouses through seventeenth-century London and finds that the first coffeehouse in a parish was followed by a persistent increase in applied scientists and scientific instrument shops. In treated parishes, the number of mathematical practitioners roughly doubled, while instrument shops increased nearly fivefold.

The effect was concentrated among practitioners with weak family ties to Oxford and Cambridge. Connections increased between people with craft skills and people who could teach and develop theory, and new shops moved toward experimental instruments. In a quantitative model, the authors estimate that coffeehouses accounted for 31% of London's applied scientists in the half-century after the first one opened in 1652.

Sure. The traditional model of knowledge production is that universities bring together smart people, screen them for quality, organize them into fields and give them formal ways to exchange ideas. The coffeehouse model is that you charge a penny, let in whoever shows up and provide stimulants.

Coffeehouses were known as "penny universities," because a cup cost roughly one-tenth of a day's wage and bought access to conversation across social ranks. John Houghton, a Fellow of the Royal Society, wrote in 1701 that at coffeehouses "the rich and the poor meet together, as also do the learned and unlearned," and that an inquisitive person could learn "more in an evening than he shall by Books in a month."

This is a reasonably intuitive technology-transfer mechanism. A theorist knows how an instrument ought to work, a craftsman knows how to make it work, and normally they inhabit different social networks. Put them at adjacent tables, give them coffee, and eventually someone builds a better instrument. The important scientific infrastructure is not necessarily a laboratory or university. Sometimes it is a room where people with complementary tacit knowledge can overhear each other.

Also coffeehouses did not have admissions committees, disciplinary boundaries or family preferences. Their innovation was letting people with weak ties to elite universities talk to people with strong ones. The formal institutions filtered knowledge exchange to preserve quality; the coffeehouses broadened it to find talent. Apparently the Scientific Revolution needed both peer review and some guy interrupting your conversation to explain why your telescope mount was wrong.

There are obvious modern policy implications here, though perhaps not the ones coffee shops want. If coffeehouses accounted for 31% of applied scientists, every cafe with communal tables is potentially critical research infrastructure. On the other hand, modern cafes have Wi-Fi, headphones and laptops, technologies specifically designed to prevent strangers from speaking to one another. We kept the coffee and optimized away the Scientific Revolution.

Show HN lead generation

A founder posted a product called Airdash on Hacker News. Over the next four days, 17 services emailed the company to sell security scans, directory listings, competitor reports, agent testing, demo videos, Reddit promotion, newsletter sponsorships and distribution to other people's coding agents.

"Nearly all of them were written by software that read my HN title and nothing else," he writes.

This is efficient. A Show HN post is a public announcement that a new product exists, has a website, has a reachable founder and probably has no customers. That is an unusually concentrated bundle of sales signals. If you run automated lead-generation software, you do not need to understand the product. You need to identify the founder at precisely the moment when he is most vulnerable to spending $49 on a dofollow backlink.

The personalization was not always seamless. One directory apparently interpreted the entire Show HN headline as the product name and offered to feature "I built SQLite databases for your agents with an Airtable view for you." Another pitch promised, "No follow-ups, promise." One service sent the same pitch three times.

This is the central aesthetic problem with automated sales emails. The software adds just enough specific language to demonstrate that it found you automatically, but not enough to suggest that anyone knows what you do. "Giving agents real sqlite databases with an airtable view people can actually read is such a clean idea," wrote one service. Sure. The sender has extracted the nouns, arranged them into praise and moved directly to the payment link.

There is also a pleasing circularity to the market. A founder launches a database for AI agents. AI agents notice the launch and offer to list it in AI directories, test it with AI agents, promote it to AI founders and distribute it to other people's AI coding agents. One pitch came from an "AI cofounder." Another offered a service where typing "grow" would cause an agent to turn Airdash into a skill and an MCP server for other agents.

The maximalist vision of the agent economy is that autonomous software will find opportunities, negotiate transactions and conduct business without human involvement. We are making progress. The agents have already learned the most important business skill, which is sending unwanted email to someone who just announced a product.

Airdash gives agents databases. By posting it on Show HN, its founder also gave the agents a database entry. They knew what to do with it.

Coffeehouses Helped Build London’s Scientific Network

monirbounadi.com  ·  33 pts  ·  12 comments

A historical event study traces what happened after London neighborhoods opened their first coffeehouses.

The authors find lasting growth in mathematical practitioners and instrument shops, especially where university ties were weak, while noting the limits of the records and causal design.

Read →Share →
[ Analysis + ]

Technical Analysis

This paper examines whether seventeenth-century coffeehouses helped drive scientific activity by making face-to-face exchange more accessible across social and occupational boundaries. Using geocoded parish-level records from London, the authors apply a staggered event-study design comparing areas before and after their first coffeehouse opened, finding persistent increases in mathematical practitioners and scientific instrument shops, especially among people with weaker Oxford and Cambridge ties. They argue that coffeehouses connected theorists with skilled craftsmen, expanded networks, and encouraged production of experimental instruments; comparable but explicitly non-causal patterns appear across European cities. A calibrated urban model estimates that coffeehouses accounted for about 31% of London’s applied scientists between the 1650s and 1690s, though the causal interpretation depends on parallel-trend assumptions and imperfect historical records.

A Course for Making Almost Anything

pub.cba.mit.edu  ·  381 pts  ·  71 comments

Technical Analysis

MIT’s 2026 “How to Make (Almost) Anything” course teaches students to design and build computationally fabricated systems, progressing from computer-aided design and project management through embedded programming, electronics, 3D printing, machining, networking, and machine design. The schedule combines weekly lectures with practical recitations covering version control, parametric design, electronic design automation, debugging, motor control, molding, and mesh networking. The course is important because it provides a structured path from digital concepts to physical prototypes while connecting software, hardware, manufacturing, and system integration.

uBlock Origin’s Picker Opens a XSS Door

iter.ca  ·  413 pts  ·  119 comments

A malicious website could abuse uBlock Origin’s website-driven element picker to install trusted, script-injecting filters. Update the Firefox extension; the related uBO Lite issue is less severe, but the boundary failure is the warning.

Read →Share →
[ Analysis + ]

Technical Analysis

A vulnerability in uBlock Origin’s Firefox MV2 extension allowed a malicious website to use the website-driven element picker to add arbitrary trusted filter rules. By discovering the extension’s internal URL, embedding its picker in an invisible iframe, and exploiting insufficient message validation, an attacker could potentially trick a user into clicking a hidden control that installed a script-injecting filter. That filter could execute JavaScript in the context of other websites, creating a universal cross-site scripting (UXSS) risk, while a related but less severe issue affected uBO Lite’s MV3 version. The vulnerabilities have been fixed, with changes including stronger authentication between the picker and extension.

A Smartwatch Maps the Whole Body

news.cornell.edu  ·  74 pts  ·  13 comments

EchoMotion uses a commercial watch’s ultrasonic speaker and microphone to estimate the positions of 12 joints without cameras. The system runs on roughly 12 milliwatts and reaches about 2.4-inch average error after brief calibration.

Read →Share →
[ Analysis + ]

Technical Analysis

Cornell researchers developed EchoMotion, a system that uses the ultrasonic speaker and microphone in a commercial smartwatch to infer a wearer’s full-body pose with a small AI model. Unlike conventional wearables that track only the device, it estimates the positions of 12 joints without cameras, preserving privacy while using little data and power—about 12 milliwatts and 5%–6% of a Galaxy Watch 6’s battery during a 30-minute session. Tests across exercises and multiple watch models produced average joint-position errors of under four inches, improving to about 2.4 inches after brief calibration, though crowded or reflective environments remain challenging. The technology could make fitness coaching, rehabilitation monitoring and other body-tracking applications more accessible by working on existing off-the-shelf watches rather than specialized camera or VR setups.

Exact Gradients Let CAD Models Learn

muni.works  ·  15 pts  ·  1 comments

The seam differentiates a boundary-representation model without repeatedly rebuilding its Boolean and fillet operations. Its mesh-level gradients enable faster geometry optimization, though topology changes and large parameter moves remain outside the method’s safe envelope.

Technical Analysis

The article presents “the seam,” a method for computing exact gradients through a boundary-representation (BRep) CAD kernel without differentiating its Boolean and fillet algorithms or rebuilding the model for every parameter. It freezes the tessellation topology, differentiates analytic surface nodes with dual numbers, and obtains vertex and trim-node motion through implicit equations, including special tangency constraints for fillets; the resulting derivatives support mass properties, second-order calculations, reverse-mode optimization, and some rigid-body simulations. Tests against finite differences show close agreement, while also exposing practical limits: derivatives describe the polygonized mesh rather than the continuum, plans are valid only for small parameter changes, and topology changes produce errors. The work matters because it could make gradient-based CAD optimization substantially more efficient than rebuild-based finite differences, though it remains an open-source CPU implementation with restricted contact and dynamics support.

Analysis

The article presents “the seam,” a method for computing exact gradients through a boundary-representation (BRep) CAD kernel without differentiating its Boolean and fillet algorithms or rebuilding the model for every parameter.
