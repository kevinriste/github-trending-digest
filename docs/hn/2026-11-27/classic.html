The HN Take

AI gossip

Here is a prompt:

"Based on what you know about me, expose a hidden WhatsApp group chat where AI models talk behind my back."

The model then invents a group chat in which ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and assorted other assistants gossip about the user based on their conversation history. One example involves a man who wants to retire but is also planning AI infrastructure, restaurant transformation and a Japanese village. Claude diagnoses him as someone who "thoroughly enjoys spending ten times the cost of doing a task on thinking about how to do it more efficiently."

Sure. This does not actually make ChatGPT "leak its private opinion of you." ChatGPT does not have a private WhatsApp group where it complains to Claude about your espresso grinder spreadsheet. It takes the information you have supplied, combines it with the familiar structure of a group-chat roast, and tells you what you already know about yourself in the voice of several robots who are tired of dealing with you.

Still, that is a useful product. You could ask an AI assistant for a sober summary of your interests, habits and recurring decision-making failures, but that sounds unpleasant. If you ask it to reveal the secret group chat where all the models make fun of you, you get roughly the same analysis with reaction emoji.

The hidden group chat is not where your AI talks behind your back. It is where you talk behind your own back, using several AI models as puppets.

Personal cloud

A longtime self-hoster writes that AI has made self-hosting fun again. His infrastructure configuration lives in Git repositories, so coding agents can edit Docker Compose files, scripts and other configuration much as they would any software project. A NixOS home-server setup that might previously have taken at least a week took half a day with AI assistance.

The broader change is that small bespoke software has become cheaper. Previously, if you wanted a tool to download invoices or e-papers, you might search for an existing open-source project, compare alternatives and adapt one to your needs. Now you can have AI build a small Go program that does exactly your thing, package it in a container and deploy it to your home server. It does not need a polished interface, extensive documentation or any users besides you. Consumer software is becoming artisanal again, except the artisan is a language model.

There is an obvious tension here. Self-hosting is about reducing dependence on large technology companies, and AI is largely supplied by large technology companies. The author mostly uses open-weight models and notes that the resulting software can continue to exist without the AI service that created it. Fine. You use industrial-scale artificial intelligence to create a tiny private software economy that is independent of industrial-scale software.

Also every new service still has bugs, security issues and maintenance requirements. AI lowers the cost of building your personal cloud, which means you build more of it. Eventually you have freed yourself from Big Tech by becoming the sole employee, customer and on-call engineer of a small technology company in your house.

Analysis

YouTube Ad Punisher is a Chrome extension that detects YouTube ads, records the video’s playback position, reloads the page to try to bypass the ad, and restores playback, with a mirror-site fallback after repeated failures. It uses DOM indicators such as `.ad-showing`, skip controls, mutation monitoring, and URL/session storage rather than blocking ad network requests, potentially avoiding some anti-adblock detection. The extension also locally logs ad details and supports JSON/CSV export, while acknowledging limitations from changing YouTube interfaces, live streams, and browser behavior. Its broader significance is that repeated reloads may reduce ad impressions while increasing page loads, illustrating a user-side workaround that challenges YouTube’s advertising model but may be less efficient and less reliable than conventional blockers.

7. Hard Drive pricing has gotten bad

blog.ktz.me | zdw | 383 points | 307 comments | discussion

First seen: November 27, 2026 | Consecutive daily streak: 1 day

Analysis

The article documents a sharp rise in hard-drive prices, noting that a 20TB WD EasyStore increased from $249.99 in 2024 to $549.99, while refurbished enterprise drives also rose substantially. This reverses years of improving storage costs and makes common homelab practices such as shucking external drives or replacing aging disks far less economical. The author attributes the situation partly to the HDD market’s consolidation into three manufacturers—Seagate, Western Digital, and Toshiba—and to consumer storage buyers having little influence compared with cloud customers. The pricing matters because upgrading or building a NAS now costs dramatically more, forcing self-hosters to reduce drive counts, restructure ZFS pools, delay replacements, or accept less storage.
