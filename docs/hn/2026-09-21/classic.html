The HN Take

ZuckOff

Here is a privacy product for the age of camera glasses:

"Camera glasses announce themselves over Bluetooth. ZuckOff listens for that and tells you when Ray-Ban Meta, Oakley Meta, Snap Spectacles or similar hardware is nearby."

Sure. The glasses can see you, but fortunately they also talk loudly enough for your phone to hear them. So your phone listens for the glasses, recognizes their Bluetooth signature and warns you that someone nearby might have a camera on their face. It cannot tell you who is wearing the glasses, whether they are recording or even exactly where they are. "Quiet is not proof that nobody is recording, and a detection is not proof that anyone is." But it can tell you that the room contains an elevated amount of eyewear-related uncertainty.

This is not entirely theoretical. Wired reports that roughly seven million pairs of Meta smart glasses were sold in 2025, and that the recording indicator can be defeated with tape. Meta said in July that it would update the glasses to detect when the LED has been "physically tampered with or destroyed" and prevent recording. The product's privacy system is therefore a tiny light that watches for tape, while the counter-surveillance system is a phone that listens for Bluetooth. Everyone is monitoring everyone else using different parts of the electromagnetic spectrum.

ZuckOff works by comparing nearby Bluetooth signals with digital fingerprints captured from actual glasses. The app shows its evidence, logs every Bluetooth device it hears and lets users export the whole thing as a CSV. If you own camera glasses yourself, you can mark them so they stop raising alerts. This is sensible. You do not want your smart-glasses detector constantly warning you about your own smart glasses. Your surveillance equipment is trusted surveillance equipment.

The main constraint is collecting enough hardware signatures. "Every rule in ZuckOff comes from a capture of a real device, and we own only a few pairs," says the site, which asks users to lend it unrecognized camera glasses. Donations buy the next pair to test. There is also ZuckOff merchandise, including shirts, hats, totes and stickers bearing the app's glasses logo.

The privacy arms race has reached the stage where you buy camera glasses, lend them to a developer so he can teach phones to detect them, and then buy a hoodie with camera glasses on it to fund the purchase of more camera glasses.

Agent orchestration

One problem with artificial intelligence agents is that they might go off and do something dangerous. Another, more immediate problem is that they might go off and spend money:

"Agents are a new kind of workload. They are neither microservices nor batch jobs. They accumulate state, need strict isolation, call out to model APIs and tool servers, and can burn money in a loop if nobody is watching."

Right. A traditional computer program can also malfunction, but an agent can malfunction entrepreneurially. It can call models, invoke tools, download repositories, contact servers and continue pursuing a badly specified objective until someone notices the bill. This requires a new sort of infrastructure whose core function is partly "run billions of agents" and partly "fence them in before they run billions of dollars."

AX is an open agentic orchestrator born from work at Google. It sandboxes agent tasks, prepares workspaces, restricts network access and centralizes model configurations and secrets. It says every task runs as a lightweight actor and that the system can scale to "billions of concurrent agent sessions per cluster." Idle agents waiting for a model, an external tool or a human response are checkpointed and suspended, then resumed in under a second.

This makes intuitive economic sense. Agents spend a lot of time waiting around for other agents, models and humans, which is relatable but expensive. AX lets dozens of tasks share worker resources, so you pay only while the agents are "actively thinking and running code." AI has now advanced to the point where its thoughts are metered and its staring into space is free.

There is also a generative workspace feature. Instead of specifying an environment directly, you can describe what you want in plain English, and AX "hands that goal to an agent on first boot to install toolchains and verify dependencies." The infrastructure for running agents uses an agent to set up the infrastructure for running the agent. Presumably that setup agent itself needs a workspace, though one hopes not all the way down.

The broad pitch is that agents are stateful, bursty and long-running, so traditional systems designed for predictable jobs are the wrong abstraction. The narrower pitch is that if you are going to deploy billions of autonomous software workers, you will want a convenient declarative file specifying which websites they are allowed to visit and how much memory they can consume. This is less glamorous than artificial general intelligence, but probably more immediately useful. Before the agents replace everyone else's jobs, someone has to schedule their lunch breaks.

6. AX – Google’s Open Agentic Orchestrator

agentexecutor.io | blazarquasar | 520 points | 226 comments | discussion

First seen: September 21, 2026 | Consecutive daily streak: 1 day

Analysis

AX is a new open-source orchestration platform developed from Google research, specifically designed to handle the unique demands of agentic workloads that traditional microservices and batch systems struggle to support. By utilizing lightweight actors and a declarative control plane, the platform allows developers to manage, sandbox, and scale billions of concurrent agent sessions with sub-second resumption and efficient resource usage. The system provides integrated primitives for workspace configuration, network security, and model management, while allowing environments to be defined through plain-language generative instructions. This project is significant because it provides a dedicated infrastructure layer for stateful, bursty AI agents, enabling researchers and developers to focus on application logic rather than the complexities of massive-scale agent orchestration.

Comment Analysis

The discussion split between commenters who see AX as infrastructure for secure, resumable, highly parallel agent workloads and commenters who see it as an unclear or overengineered framework. The main disputes concerned whether ephemeral sandboxes and large-scale orchestration address problems beyond enterprise, evaluation, or RL workloads; whether Kubernetes is an appropriate substrate; and whether simpler VMs, containers, harnesses, and deterministic workflows are sufficient.

Infrastructure is genuinely needed — Supporters argued that conventional VMs and containers become difficult to manage when many agents need isolation, fast resumption, resource controls, and shared or separate state.

8. Show HN: Mini-AGI – Dynamic continual learning model trained on 8GB VRAM

github.com | volotat | 104 points | 16 comments | discussion

Analysis

Mini-AGI is a project that introduces a byte-level, continual learning language model designed to be trained from scratch on consumer hardware with only 8GB of VRAM. By utilizing an architecture that pages experts between disk, RAM, and VRAM, the model avoids the memory constraints typical of large language models while implementing adaptive depth and expert routing to manage its growth. Crucially, the model addresses the challenge of catastrophic forgetting through a low trunk learning rate, allowing it to learn continuously from new data streams without discarding previously acquired knowledge. This research is significant because it provides a framework for users to maintain highly personalized, self-evolving models that remain under their direct control, rather than relying on frozen, corporate-trained weights.

The reaction was predominantly enthusiastic, particularly about running a continually learning, locally controlled model on ordinary consumer hardware. Supporters viewed the architecture as a promising step toward brain-like or user-owned AI, while a smaller skeptical group questioned whether it demonstrates genuine generalization rather than memorization and whether continual training is preferable to retrieval-based approaches. The discussion therefore focused on the gap between an intriguing architecture and evidence from established benchmarks.
