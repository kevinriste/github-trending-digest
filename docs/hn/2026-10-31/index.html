PhD parliament

Serbia has elected what may be the world's most academically qualified legislature:

After a student-led opposition movement won in Serbia on 25 October, the group, named Student List - Students Are Winning, has 141 representatives in Serbia's 250-member National Assembly. Dozens of them come from the academic world, and more than half have a Ph.D. or M.D.

The new representatives include an astrophysicist, an electrical engineer who has designed robots, an organic chemist who worked on Covid-19 drugs, physicians, economists, sociologists and political scientists. The students themselves largely did not run. They assembled a list of "trusted figures," including academics, farmers, lawyers, artists, teachers and a former basketball player.

This is a pretty intuitive response to a political system that you think is corrupt: Replace the politicians with people who have demonstrated expertise and professional credibility. If you distrust career politicians, perhaps you would prefer the organic chemist who spent the pandemic working on drugs. She at least has experience solving problems where the underlying reality gets a vote.

There are some risks. Many of the new representatives have no political experience, and being good at studying galaxy evolution does not necessarily make you good at assembling a parliamentary coalition. Though arguably galaxy evolution is useful preparation for watching large unstable bodies collide over extremely long periods of time.

The movement has promised meritocracy, accountability, academic autonomy and more predictable science funding after 13 years of rule by Aleksandar Vucic. Researchers hope it will reverse political interference in universities and research grants. One physicist said that funding had often been distributed based on "political or personal connections rather than merit," which is a bad system for science and, traditionally, a reasonably standard system for politics.

Serbian lawmakers are allowed to keep outside jobs where there is no conflict of interest, so some of the academics might remain academics. This is efficient. If parliament gets bogged down, they can return to the university and study why.

Airplane mode

Vitalik Buterin has a theory of digital minimalism. The idea is not necessarily to stop using digital technology, but to make each use intentional rather than letting an algorithm decide what you should stare at.

His main example is X. He did not leave the platform; he left its interface, posting and reading mostly through Firefly, which combines X, Bluesky, Farcaster and Lens. Firefly is missing one important feature: "The Algorithm."

Buterin describes the basic algorithmic loop. You see a post supporting Adolf Hitler, click on it out of morbid curiosity, and the algorithm concludes: "This user seems to be a fan of Adolf Hitler." It then shows you 10 Hitler posts. You stare at those because seeing 10 burning cars is even more arresting than seeing one burning car. The algorithm updates its estimate of your interests and shows you 100.

This is a familiar problem with engagement optimization. The computer cannot reliably distinguish "I love this" from "this is so appalling that I cannot look away." From the computer's perspective that distinction is sentimental. You clicked.

Buterin's other experiment was to keep his phone on airplane mode for 100 days, while continuing to use WiFi. This improved location privacy and forced him to spend meaningful periods without internet access. It also meant that in Singapore he could not receive the SMS code required to log into Grab, so he had fewer food options and sometimes had to take the subway instead of a car.

This is perhaps the purest form of digital self-sovereignty: No telecom company can continuously track your location, no app can summon dinner to your door, and you are free to carry yourself physically to a restaurant like an anonymous cryptographic primitive.

How GCC Chooses a Switch Strategy

xoranth.net  ·  67 pts  ·  6 comments

A C or C++ switch statement can become a jump table, a bitset, or a branching comparison tree. GCC weighs density, code size, architecture, and probability—then uses dynamic programming to assemble the profitable pieces.

Read →Share →
[ Analysis + ]

Technical Analysis

The article explains how GCC lowers C/C++ `switch` statements into machine code, comparing jump tables, bitset tests, and binary comparison trees. It shows that GCC uses heuristics based on case density, contiguous ranges, duplicate destinations, architecture-specific costs, optimization-for-speed versus size, and profile probabilities to choose among these strategies. The compiler can isolate dense subsets for jump tables and use bitsets for compact ranges with few outcomes, while falling back to probability-aware decision trees when those approaches are not worthwhile. The article then traces these decisions through GCC’s `pass_lower_switch`, including dynamic-programming algorithms that find profitable clusters, making the implementation’s performance and code-size tradeoffs understandable.

A Nintendo Wii Boots Classic Mac OS

virtuallyfun.com  ·  311 pts  ·  90 comments

The GameCube and Wii were never Apple machines, but their PowerPC hardware provides an unexpected home for Mac OS 7.6. Reverse-engineered firmware, memory maps, interrupts, and drivers turn a familiar console into a working window onto computing history.

Read →Share →
[ Analysis + ]

Technical Analysis

The story details an enthusiast’s effort to port classic PowerPC Mac OS to Nintendo’s GameCube and Wii hardware, whose ArtX-derived chipsets differ substantially from Apple’s systems. The work involves adapting OpenBIOS and the Mac OS boot process, patching assumptions about memory maps, interrupt controllers, power management, device trees, and missing Apple hardware, and writing display, controller, and storage drivers. After extensive reverse engineering and debugging, Mac OS 7.6 reached a usable desktop on real GameCube hardware, while later versions exposed additional compatibility issues. The project is significant as a technically novel demonstration of running historically specific software on unsupported hardware, while also documenting the engineering challenges and limitations of preserving classic Mac OS outside Apple’s ecosystem.

A Rust Tool Challenges Cloudflare’s Gate

github.com  ·  220 pts  ·  64 comments

`cfsolve` obtains Turnstile tokens and clearance cookies without launching a headless browser. It emulates supported browser profiles from the command line or embedded Rust, but its fingerprints—and its usefulness—remain vulnerable to the next change in the challenge system.

Read →Share →
[ Analysis + ]

Technical Analysis

Cloudflare’s `cfsolve` is a Rust library and command-line tool that obtains Turnstile tokens and `cf_clearance` cookies without using a headless browser. It emulates supported browser profiles—currently including iOS Safari and Chrome/Chromium—using recorded challenge responses, and supports proxies, custom HTML, widgets, and challenge pages. Developers can use it as a CLI or embed it in Rust applications, but must keep the resulting cookie tied to the profile’s user agent and IP address. The project is significant because it offers a lightweight, automatable approach to bypassing Cloudflare access checks, while its dependence on browser fingerprints and manually produced profiles may limit reliability as challenges change.
