The HN Take

Decision model.

Here is a nice idea: If you want an artificial intelligence model to choose among several options, perhaps you should ask it which option it prefers. Not ask it to write a little essay in JSON about which option it prefers. Just look.

OpenJev is a browser-only experiment that runs a local model and compares two ways of getting a probability distribution over choices. One way reads the model's logits for the supplied options and normalizes them. The other asks the same model to generate the probabilities token by token as JSON.

"One reads option probabilities directly; the other asks the model to write its option probabilities as JSON text."

Right. Imagine that you ask an employee whether the company should do A, B or C. One approach is to read her answer. Another is to ask her to prepare a properly formatted regulatory filing describing the probability that she would answer A, B or C, and then infer her answer from that. The filing might be useful for some purposes, but it introduces a lot of ways to get the answer wrong.

Large language models are trained to predict tokens. If you supply options corresponding to tokens, the model already has scores for them. You can just read those scores. Or you can ask the model to turn the scores into decimal numbers, put the numbers in JSON, make them add up properly and stop generating text at the right moment. This is approximately using an AI model to predict what an AI model thinks, with the second AI model being the same AI model but wearing an accounting visor.

The experiment is pleasingly literal. It downloads a quantized local model, runs both methods sequentially on the same GPU and reports the timing. The direct method has "1 readout." The generation method has a first token, total time, input tokens and output tokens. You can watch the model slowly compose a written report about information that was already sitting inside it.

There are limitations. The direct scores are "a softmax over only the displayed option tokens." They are not calibrated confidence, and they exclude answers the model might prefer that you did not offer. If you ask whether the correct investment is A, B or C, and the model's actual answer is "please stop," OpenJev will still allocate 100% among A, B and C. But this is also how many human decision processes work.

The demo runs locally, with no backend, and its model weights remain in the browser cache. The default desktop model is a 1.56-gigabyte MiniCPM5 2B model; larger models require more memory. There is no waitlist. At last, democratized access to asking a machine what it thinks before asking it to explain what it thinks.

Theft memo.

There is a conventional public argument about training artificial intelligence models on copyrighted material. AI companies say that training is transformative fair use, that models learn statistical patterns rather than storing works, and that the resulting products create new value. Publishers say that the companies copied their work without permission and built products that compete with it.

And then there is the internal PowerPoint version. Newly unredacted material in the New York Times's copyright lawsuit against OpenAI and Microsoft quotes a Microsoft executive calling AI training "an astonishing theft of unprecedented proportions" and "the largest theft of labor in human history."

To be fair, the quotes are presented in the Times's brief without their original context, because the underlying exhibits remain sealed. Perhaps the missing context is: "Some uninformed person might inaccurately call this the largest theft of labor in human history, but I certainly would not." Lawyers will investigate.

Still, the quoted internal analysis is admirably direct. Microsoft data showed that its Copilot answer engine reduced click-through rates to the Times's domain by as much as 93% compared with traditional Bing search. An internal presentation called this a "doom loop" that would "hurt the performance of our models and the entire web at the same time."

"It is highly unusual that an end-product threatens the economic foundations of its essential suppliers," the document said, "but that is the situation we have created for our LLM business with respect to its 'content supply chain.'"

Yes. The basic AI content business is:

Publishers pay journalists to produce useful information.

AI companies copy the useful information.

AI products answer readers' questions so they do not visit the publishers.

Publishers lose the money they use to pay journalists.

Eventually there is less useful information to copy.

This is bad supply-chain management. If your factory depends on a supplier of specialized components, you generally do not build a product whose key feature is that nobody pays the supplier anymore. Or perhaps you do, but internally you call it a doom loop.

The filing also says that OpenAI employees discussed a "hack to get around nytimes paywall." When a researcher mentioned it to OpenAI President Greg Brockman, he allegedly replied: "ah nice."

Again, context may matter. But "ah nice" is an unusually compact corporate approval process.

Meanwhile OpenAI's head of ChatGPT reportedly wrote that publishers face an "existential threat" from chatbots, which are "largely substitutive" and will become more substitutive as they improve. This is awkward for a fair-use argument that considers whether the new use harms the market for the original. The public position is that the product transforms journalism. The private concern is that it substitutes for journalism so effectively that journalism disappears and takes the model's training data with it.

There is a certain grandiosity to calling web scraping the largest theft of labor in human history. Human history has had some fairly competitive thefts of labor. But perhaps the claim is quantitative: more than 2 million documents from nytimes.com in one Common Crawl-derived dataset, at least 160,903 unique publisher works in Project Mango, and more than 91,692 copies of works in OpenAI's mid-training datasets. Industrial scale changes the superlative.

The AI industry often says that data is the new oil. Apparently the complete analogy is that you extract it, remove the identifying material, damage the surrounding ecosystem and then hire lawyers to explain why this is economically necessary.

1. OpenJev

First seen: September 18, 2026 | Consecutive daily streak: 1 day

Analysis

OpenJev is a browser-based tool that allows users to run local decision-making models directly within their web browser using WebGPU, ensuring that all data processing remains private and client-side. The project provides a comparative interface to evaluate two different methods of model decision-making: reading direct probability logits versus generating probabilities through tokenized JSON output. By utilizing quantized models like MiniCPM and Qwen, the tool offers a transparent way to measure performance differences between these approaches on local hardware without requiring a backend server. This experiment highlights the growing feasibility of running complex, privacy-focused machine learning tasks directly in the browser, providing researchers and developers with a sandbox for benchmarking model behavior.

3. The scourge of x86 emulation

fex-emu.com | dagmx | 160 points | 28 comments | discussion

First seen: September 18, 2026 | Consecutive daily streak: 1 day

Analysis

This article from the FEX emulation team explores the technical challenges of emulating the strict x86 Total Store Ordering (x86-TSO) memory model on the weak memory model inherent to ARM architectures. Because x86 applications frequently rely on strong memory consistency and disregard alignment, emulators must bridge the gap using costly barrier instructions or newer ARM extensions like LRCPC, which often result in significant performance overheads. The author highlights that while ARM hardware vendors are slowly adding features to support these requirements, Apple Silicon’s hardware-level support for TSO remains the current gold standard for minimizing emulation latency. Understanding these memory model discrepancies is vital for developers aiming to achieve native-like performance when running x86-based software on modern ARM-based processors.

Commenters also considered bytecode and install-time recompilation, but emphasized backward compatibility, native-code-heavy games, tooling, and ecosystem constraints. Apple’s x86-compatible memory ordering was viewed as a significant advantage, though some commenters cautioned that it is neither a complete nor simple solution.
