Hacker curation

The internet has discovered a new recommendation algorithm: Hacker News, but again.

HackerBooks collects books mentioned in Hacker News comments, with a daily shelf drawn from 15,766 titles. The list includes Das Kapital, Finnegans Wake, Effective Java, The Hunger Games and Learn You a Haskell for Great Good!, which seems like a plausible reconstruction of the average Hacker News user's bedside table.

This follows HackerTube, which collected 6,454 YouTube videos recommended by Hacker News commenters after its creator encountered an "enshittified wall of slop" on YouTube. He asked Claude Opus 5.5 to download the comments, filter them and build a replacement. "This takes ~2 hours now," he writes.

There is a sensible idea here. YouTube has an enormous amount of good content and a recommendation system optimized for YouTube's purposes. Hacker News commenters have spent almost 20 years manually identifying good videos and books for their own purposes. So you scrape their recommendations and build a new recommendation system. The algorithm is people complaining in comments.

And then HackerBooks was recommended on Hacker News, where it became the top post. Hacker News recommended a website containing things recommended by Hacker News, and presumably tomorrow someone will build a website recommending Hacker News websites recommended by Hacker News. Eventually the entire internet will be a nicely organized index of Hacker News comments. The comments will complain about it.

Bug productivity

Here is one way artificial intelligence can make software development slower:

PostgreSQL 19 will probably ship in November, later than its usual September-to-October window, after several large features were removed late in beta. The reason, writes PostgreSQL developer Robert Haas, is that LLMs have made finding bugs "vastly easier." Fixing them has also become easier, "but not to the same degree."

Between feature freeze and Sept. 25, back-patched commits to the release branch increased by more than 60% from 2025 to 2026. Back-patches to the prior release increased by more than 74%. People are using LLMs to find old and new PostgreSQL bugs, and human maintainers have to review and fix them. "Those humans have not gotten any smarter between 2025 and 2026."

Sure. The standard AI productivity story is that a developer asks an AI to write code, the AI writes it quickly, and the developer ships more software. But software maintenance is a queue. If AI increases the arrival rate of plausible bug reports faster than it increases the service rate of trusted human review, the queue gets longer and releases get later. More productivity!

PostgreSQL has roughly 40 years of code containing bugs that humans did not find. Those bugs were economically harmless in the sense that nobody knew about them. LLMs have now discovered a vast reserve of previously inaccessible bug deposits and made them commercially recoverable. This is good for software quality, eventually. In the meantime the maintainers have to mine all of them.

Technical Analysis

PostgreSQL 19 is likely to ship in November or later because LLM-assisted testing and bug discovery have exposed far more defects across both the development and supported release branches. How Berkeley Began Building BSD

fosscomics.com  ·  145 pts  ·  15 comments

In 1977, Berkeley’s first BSD release bundled Pascal and ex atop an AT&T Unix license. The illustrated history follows the small tools, patient ports, and practical distribution work that carried Berkeley Unix toward 3BSD and DARPA’s attention.

Read →Share →
[ Analysis + ]

Technical Analysis

The story traces BSD Unix’s origins at Berkeley, where Bill Joy and Chuck Haley began improving the Unix kernel and packaging software for distribution to other universities. The first BSD release in 1977 bundled the Pascal system and ex, while 2BSD added vi, the C shell, and other tools; these were software collections requiring an existing AT&T Unix license, not standalone operating systems. Berkeley’s low-cost, research-oriented sharing model helped spread Unix, while Joy personally handled much of the copying and distribution. After Berkeley integrated and debugged virtual memory support for the VAX and ported its tools, 3BSD emerged in 1979, demonstrating Unix’s portability and attracting DARPA’s interest as a way to connect research institutions using diverse hardware.

Bones Signal the Body to Age Slowly

science.org  ·  132 pts  ·  30 comments

In mice, mechanically stressed bone cells release factors including PEDF that improve organ health and extend late-life survival.

The result offers a plausible biological account of exercise’s benefits, while leaving human efficacy and safety firmly unresolved.

Read →Share →
[ Analysis + ]

Technical Analysis

Researchers found in mice that mechanical stress on bones activates PIEZO1-sensing osteocytes, which release proteins—including PEDF—that appear to improve organ health, cognition, immune function, and longevity. Disabling this pathway accelerated aging, while gently loading the leg bones or using a PIEZO1-activating drug extended lifespan by nearly three months in old mice. The work offers a potential biological explanation for some benefits of exercise and points toward future therapies based on bone-derived factors rather than exercise alone. However, the findings have not been demonstrated in humans, and substantial research is needed to establish whether bone loading or treatments targeting PEDF are safe and effective.

fz-juelich.de  ·  151 pts  ·  68 comments

Technical Analysis

Researchers led by Forschungszentrum Jülich traced regular, one-minute dips in European and global power-grid frequency to large battery-storage systems performing synchronized state-of-charge checks at the start of each minute. Measurements from 80 devices on the Jülich campus confirmed that these checks briefly increase electricity consumption, creating a grid-wide signal when many systems act simultaneously. Though currently not a critical security issue, the fluctuations can make hydroelectric turbines repeatedly slow and accelerate, increasing wear, and could grow as battery storage and data centers expand. The researchers say manufacturers could largely mitigate the problem by randomizing check times, while noting that other digitally controlled appliances may contribute similar synchronization effects.
