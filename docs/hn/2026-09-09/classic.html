The HN Take

Quantum night shift

Here is a sensible use of an AI agent:

MIT graduate student Beatriz Yankelevich connected GPT-5.6 Sol, through Codex, to laboratory software used to characterize superconducting qubits. The agent chose measurement parameters, operated the hardware, analyzed the data and decided whether to refine a measurement or move to the next one.

This is not quite "the AI built a quantum computer." The chip had already been fabricated, packaged and cooled to near absolute zero. But once that was done, researchers interacted with it entirely through software, which means that much of the remaining work was clicking buttons at an extremely advanced physics laboratory. AI is coming for everyone's clicking buttons.

Qubit calibration requires hundreds or thousands of preliminary measurements, with each result determining what to try next. On an uncalibrated six-qubit chip, Codex could identify transition frequencies, calibrate control and readout pulses, and measure how long the qubits retained quantum information. When the signals were clear, it completed the standard workflow with little intervention. When the signals were weak or noisy, it sometimes needed an experienced researcher.

So the division of labor is that the AI operates the quantum computer when quantum mechanics behaves normally, while the human steps in when quantum mechanics gets confusing. Fair enough.

"I can have agents running measurements for many hours overnight or while I'm working in the cleanroom," Yankelevich said. "I can check in from my phone, see what they've done, and steer them if something needs fixing."

A superconducting quantum chip is cooled to near absolute zero inside a dilution refrigerator, interrogated with precisely controlled microwave signals and supervised from a phone. The future of experimental physics is apparently checking whether your overnight agents got stuck while you were away from your desk.

The immediate benefit is not that the agent is better than an expert. OpenAI says experienced researchers may still find the best calibration settings faster. The benefit is that a researcher can run several days' worth of repetitive characterization without personally monitoring every step. The AI does the routine science, and the scientist does the interesting science, at least until the interesting science becomes sufficiently routine.

Millennium prize economics

Oh sure:

OpenAI says that 10,000 AI agents, working for 88 hours, found a finite-time blowup solution for the Navier-Stokes equations, potentially resolving one of the Clay Mathematics Institute's Millennium Problems. The agents produced a case in which the equations evolve to describe fluid moving at infinite speed, which is not how fluid generally behaves.

The result has not yet been confirmed. If it is, the Clay prize is $1 million. OpenAI's effort cost "millions of dollars," according to the company. An independent researcher estimated that it used about $1 million of inference compute internally, or would have cost roughly $6 million at retail rates.

This is possibly the purest imaginable AI research business model: Spend $1 million of compute to win a $1 million mathematics prize, and then spend considerably more time arguing about who deserves it. The financial return is approximately zero before salaries, but the marketing return is probably better.

The story is also a scientific priority dispute. Mathematicians Tristan Buckmaster and Levent Alpoge had spent the past year using several AI tools, including Codex and Claude, to pursue related blowup results. They obtained results for the Euler equations and other systems in August, verified one proof in Lean, and were trying to turn the output into readable mathematics.

Buckmaster described the Euler write-up as "AI slop." This is an important new stage in mathematics. Previously, producing a major proof was difficult. Now producing the proof might take a month, while formatting it into something other mathematicians can stand to read takes several more weeks.

Then rumors circulated that Anthropic might have solved major open problems. OpenAI noticed. "We have such a strong model. Why don't we try to solve also a Millennium Prize problem?" researcher Sebastien Bubeck said. OpenAI initially attacked all six unsolved Millennium Problems, found Navier-Stokes promising, ran 1,000 agents on the simpler Euler equations for 50 hours, and then escalated to 10,000 agents on Navier-Stokes.

There is a familiar theory that scientific discoveries happen when one unusually insightful person spends years staring at the right problem. The newer theory is that scientific discoveries happen when one company hears that a competing company might announce something and immediately deploys millions of dollars of compute at it. Priority disputes used to be conducted by letters sent across Europe. Now they are denial-of-service attacks on the remaining unsolved problems.

Buckmaster says OpenAI later offered a path under which he alone would write up the Navier-Stokes result, removing Alpoge, who is affiliated with Anthropic, while acknowledging that an internal OpenAI model had solved it. He declined. OpenAI says no employee or agent saw the pair's work before it became public, though its announcement reportedly said it could not rule out that de-identified product usage data had helped improve its models.

So there are at least three possible contributors to a modern mathematical breakthrough: the mathematicians who developed the underlying program, the researchers who pushed it forward with AI, and the company that saw rumors of their progress and brought 10,000 agents. Fortunately mathematics has a long tradition of resolving credit disputes calmly and quickly. Now it has agents for that too.
