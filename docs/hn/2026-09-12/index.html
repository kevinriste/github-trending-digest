Neural engine archaeology

Here is a fairly pure reverse-engineering motivation:

"I stopped working on the reverse-engineered Apple Neural Engine driver three years ago, upon a sad mini realization that the ANE block is just not that useful."

The problem was not that Apple's Neural Engine lacked arithmetic. The M1 version had 16 compute cores and 2,048 parallel multiply-accumulate lanes. It could multiply numbers and add them together at considerable speed. The problem was that it was designed around a particular idea of what machine learning would look like: dense image-processing models with predictable reuse of data.

Then machine learning decided to look different.

"A dot product is a dot product," the author writes. Convolution uses dot products; attention uses dot products. But the useful thing about specialized hardware is not just that it can do the underlying arithmetic. It is that it has made a lot of expensive physical commitments about where the numbers will come from, where they will stay and when they will move. Those commitments were sensible for the convolutional neural networks of 2017. Autoregressive transformers had other plans.

A Linux driver could expose the hardware interface, but it could not renegotiate the chip's worldview. Even macOS, the author notes, regularly uses the ANE to generate upsampled preview images in Finder. It is a specialized neural accelerator whose dependable consumer use case is making the little picture of your file look nicer.

Apple's M5 apparently acknowledges the problem by folding ANE cores into the GPU cores. The arithmetic remains useful; the surrounding architecture moves somewhere more flexible. The standalone neural processing unit starts to look like a fossil from an earlier machine-learning epoch.

So the author has returned to finish reverse-engineering the M1 ANE, because its approaching obsolescence makes it newly interesting:

"So, in honor of the ANE's apparent demise, we will do something even more useless: go back and reverse-engineer the ANE on the M1."

Sure. When the goal was to make the ANE useful, reverse-engineering it was disappointing. Now that the goal is to understand why it stopped being useful, it is perfect. The best workload for Apple's old neural accelerator turns out to be hardware archaeology, which runs on a human.

Mathematical output

An imposing group of Fields medalists has published a statement called "A Severe Misalignment of AI in Mathematics." The opening is striking:

"Over the last few months, the mathematical capabilities of LLMs have improved dramatically, to the point that they can solve major outstanding problems in many fields of mathematics."

Traditionally this would be considered good news for mathematics. Mathematics contains outstanding problems; mathematicians would like them solved; now there is apparently a machine that can solve some of them. Problem solved, in both senses.

But the mathematicians' objection is that solving problems is not actually the final product of mathematics. It is a proxy for developing understanding, methods and new questions. A famous unsolved problem functions as a landmark that organizes years of human work. Once it is solved, mathematicians spend more years checking the proof, simplifying it, identifying the new ideas, connecting them to previous work and eventually turning the result into something another human can learn.

An AI company, meanwhile, would naturally prefer a cleaner metric: unsolved yesterday, solved today. That is measurable. "Conceptual understanding gradually diffused through generations of seminars" is harder to put on a benchmark leaderboard.

The statement worries that rapid production of solutions could become a "mass production at faster and faster pace of 'true/false' statements," leaving mathematicians responsible for attribution, verification and integration. The machine produces mathematical results; humans operate the mathematical aftercare system.

Meanwhile the Clay Mathematics Institute has announced that the Navier-Stokes Millennium Prize Problem has "apparently been settled." Clay's response is excited but pointedly slow. It hopes to see "waves of new human understanding" as the work is "analysed and interrogated," and notes that its prize process is "deliberately unhurried."

That seems like the emerging division of labor. New technologies can accelerate the arrival of purported solutions to the deepest problems in mathematics. Then the mathematical community has to slow everything back down enough to find out what the solutions mean, whether they are right and who did what.

If AI becomes infinitely fast at solving mathematical problems, mathematics will not become infinitely fast. It will proceed at the speed at which mathematicians can hold talks about the answers.

Technical Analysis

This research is significant because it clarifies the often-hidden semantic trade-offs in concurrency models, helping developers navigate the complexity of their chosen language's runtime. By deconstructing these design decisions, the study provides a foundational framework for understanding the nuances of straight-line asynchrony in contemporary software development.
