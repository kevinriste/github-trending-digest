The HN Take

Common parts.

Toyota, Nissan, and Honda have discovered standardization:

Japan's three largest automakers will begin using unified quality criteria for steel, consistent protocols for plastics and resins, and a universal color-coding scheme for wiring harnesses. The idea is to reduce production costs while preserving each brand's identity.

The first areas were selected because consumers generally do not notice them. Nobody buys a Honda because its internal wiring harness uses distinctively Honda-colored wires. Or perhaps somebody does, but that person should not be directing industrial policy.

This is a fairly simple response to competition from Chinese automakers. If three companies separately specify steel, plastics and wire colors, their suppliers have to maintain three sets of processes, which costs money. If they all agree that red means red, they can buy more of the same stuff more cheaply and spend the savings on things customers care about.

"We all share this strong sense of crisis, creating a momentum for change," said Koji Sato, chairman of the Japan Automobile Manufacturers Association and vice chairman of Toyota.

Sometimes a strong sense of crisis produces mergers, factory closures or dramatic technological breakthroughs. Sometimes it produces a meeting where Toyota, Nissan and Honda agree on what color the wires should be. Progress comes in many forms.

17 million lines.

Well:

On November 20, Levent Alpoge released a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis as a Lean library comprising 17 million lines of code.

The Lean and Mathlib communities say that they have "full confidence" the proof is correct. Dozens of people compiled the library, two independent tools confirmed that it proves Mathlib's formal statement of the Riemann Hypothesis, and several proof checkers validated an inert export of the proof. Two of those checkers come with formal proofs, relative to set theory, that they cannot accept a false proof.

This is roughly the maximal version of "the computer says yes." The statement has been checked, the code compiles, the code proves the statement, the proof checks, and the proof checkers have proofs that they check proofs correctly. At some point you reach bedrock, although apparently bedrock is set theory.

Traditionally, a famous mathematical proof is circulated among experts, who read it, understand its ideas and spend months or years deciding whether it works. Here the order is different. The machine-verifiable object is 17 million lines long, the communities certified it over the course of a day, and now they "look forward to all the activity in the mathematical community that will help explain the new ideas in this proof."

First humanity proves the Riemann Hypothesis. Then humanity begins the smaller follow-up project of figuring out why.

3. A real Linux kernel, compiled to WebAssembly

github.com | DonAtimator | 47 points | 8 comments | discussion

First seen: November 22, 2026 | Consecutive daily streak: 1 day

Analysis

The open-source unkernel project compiles a real Linux 7.x kernel, including its MMU, filesystems, networking, and device support, into a WebAssembly module, while emulating only unmodified aarch64 user programs. It can boot Alpine or Debian to a shell in a browser or Node.js in under a second, with persistent storage, package managers, networking, graphics, sound, and host integration, and it offers a JavaScript SDK for embedding Linux environments in applications. Its design avoids the overhead of full-machine emulation and the compatibility limits of syscall reimplementations, delivering near-native syscall and fork performance, though CPU-heavy workloads remain slower and features such as IPv6, debugging, and full networking are limited. The project is significant because it makes a comparatively faithful, portable Linux environment deployable without servers or installation, potentially useful for browser-based development, testing, education, and embedded tooling.

4. Lean verification of the Riemann Hypothesis

First seen: November 22, 2026 | Consecutive daily streak: 1 day

Analysis

The story announces that Levent Alpöge has released a 17-million-line Lean library claiming to prove the Riemann Hypothesis, with the Lean and Mathlib communities endorsing its correctness. The proof depends only on Mathlib, compiles reproducibly, and has been checked against Mathlib’s formal statement using independent comparison tools. Its exported proof was also validated by Lean’s kernel and multiple independent proof checkers, including systems with formal soundness arguments relative to set theory. The result is important because it would provide a machine-verified proof of one of mathematics’ central unsolved problems, while shifting attention toward understanding and communicating the proof’s underlying mathematical ideas.

Analysis

The paper introduces GAMA-Bench, a benchmark of 1,298 gender-mirrored intimate and public conflict scenarios designed to test whether LLMs judge identical misconduct differently based on the actor’s gender. Across 10 models, responses to male actors were generally more punitive, directive, escalatory, and blame-focused, while responses to female actors used more therapeutic and empathetic framing, with the largest differences appearing in relationship conflicts. The study uses paired prompts, structured LLM-based scoring, alternative evaluators, and a small human-validation study to assess these response-framing gaps beyond simple bias labels or final decisions. The findings matter because AI assistants increasingly provide relationship, workplace, and conflict advice, although the results are limited by synthetic scenarios, binary gender mirroring, and reliance on model-based evaluation.

Analysis

LookAtMyQuant is an open-source quantization compiler that measures the output damage caused by compressing individual model components, then allocates bits and optionally fine-tunes weights to minimize that damage under a fixed size budget. Its reported results include a 1.23 GB Qwen3.5-4B model with competitive math performance, a 7.2 GB Qwen3.8-27B model outperforming a same-sized reference quantization on several benchmarks, and Inkling-2-Mini builds ranging from 3.85 GB to 12.76 GB. The 12.76 GB Inkling build matched its 18.89 GB BF16 teacher on the listed GSM8K, HumanEval, and vision tests, while smaller versions retained varying levels of accuracy for devices with limited memory. The project also provides Apple Silicon MLX kernels and speculative-decoding drafters, enabling high reported generation speeds, and includes reproducible compiler, training, evaluation, and runtime code under the MIT license.
