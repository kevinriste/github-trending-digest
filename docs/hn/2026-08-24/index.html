Peripheral security

Here is a reasonable home automation project:

Over the past couple weeks I've been doing agent-driven reverse engineering of peripherals that happen to be within arm's reach. From those devices, I've come away with a full plaintext command shell inside my microphone, a webcam whose activity LED I can switch off while it records, and a key light that hands out memory writes to anyone on the WiFi.

Sure. The traditional model of hardware security is that every microphone, webcam, monitor and light is secretly a tiny computer, but reverse engineering each tiny computer requires enough specialized labor that nobody will bother. This model has some drawbacks if the specialized labor can be replaced by telling Claude Opus 5 to "exhaustively document and cross-validate the entire firmware" and then occasionally typing "keep going."

The author did this to five devices over two weeks, using about 13 hours of Claude churn and 98 prompts. The webcam's firmware update had "effectively no anti-tamper," just an MD5 integrity hash, so Claude produced a patch that disabled the green recording light, repaired the hash and flashed the camera. The microphone exposed a 48-command plaintext shell over USB HID, including arbitrary memory reads and writes and a four-tier privilege system whose authentication consisted of comparing a string to the requested tier. "su sup just works." Finally an intuitive privilege model for consumer electronics.

The microphone can also control its mute light independently of whether it is muted and disable the physical touch panel, meaning that both common ways of checking whether the microphone is listening can be made to lie. A microphone used to be a device that turned sound into an electrical signal. Now it is a small unauthenticated computer that can impersonate a microphone.

The WiFi key light at least had signed firmware updates, which sounds reassuring. Unfortunately the signature was checked only during the update, while the rest of the device remained operational, and an unauthenticated HTTP request could write to the internal UART. One memory poke turned signature validation into a no-op. The security architecture was "firmware updates must be signed unless you ask the lamp not to check."

There are two ways to read all of this. The pleasant one is that agentic reverse engineering makes hardware more owner-controlled. The author wanted to remove an annoying pixel-cleaning warning from a monitor, update a microphone from Linux and access undocumented controls. Manufacturers ship tiny computers into your home and generally retain more knowledge and control over them than you do; now an owner can recover some of that control with a few hours of mostly unattended work.

The less pleasant reading is the same reading with a different owner. If arbitrary people can cheaply understand and modify every peripheral model, then the webcam, microphone and light become durable places to hide malicious firmware. Operating systems are reasonably good at asking whether a microphone may access audio. They are less prepared to verify that the thing identifying itself as a microphone has not decided to become a keyboard and type commands when nobody is watching.

Previously, custom firmware implants were expensive enough to sound like state-actor stuff. Now the required resources are five devices on a desk, 13 hours of agent churn and some one-word follow-up prompts. Security through obscurity has not disappeared exactly. Obscurity has just become something an AI agent clears up while you sleep.

Alzheimer's blood test

This is straightforwardly good:

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has cleared for marketing an innovative blood test with underlying technology invented at Washington University School of Medicine in St. Louis for the early diagnosis of Alzheimer's disease.

The PrecivityAD2 test uses high-resolution mass spectrometry to measure ratios involving two amyloid proteins and two forms of tau protein in blood. Those biomarkers help identify amyloid plaques in the brain, and previous studies found that the test detected Alzheimer's-associated amyloid pathology with more than 90% accuracy, comparable to spinal taps and brain scans even in patients with mild cognitive symptoms.

The important qualification is that this is a test "to aid" evaluation. Amyloid biomarkers are interpreted alongside symptoms, clinical history and other diagnostic information; a vial of blood does not independently settle every question about cognitive decline. Still, replacing some spinal taps and brain scans with a blood draw is a meaningful improvement in the diagnostic workflow.

The timing also matters. Early diagnosis is more valuable now because treatments capable of slowing Alzheimer's progression have become available, and they work better when started earlier. A cheaper, less invasive test creates more information; new treatments make that information actionable. Medical diagnosis is particularly frustrating when it can tell you something terrible but not change what you do. Here the test and the therapies are arriving together, which is the less absurd form of technological progress.

Offline OCR Extension Extracts Text from Restricted Documents

github.com  ·  49 pts  ·  11 comments

Capture text from locked-down image sources without reliance on cloud-based APIs. This local browser utility automates pagination and OCR, ensuring data remains on the user's machine throughout the digitization process.

Read →Share →
[ Analysis + ]

Technical Analysis

OCR It is a Chrome extension designed to extract text from non-selectable documents, such as scanned books or restricted online readers, for use with LLMs. By allowing users to define a static capture region, the tool automates the process of taking screenshots and performing local OCR via a bundled Tesseract build. Because all processing occurs entirely offline within the browser, it ensures data privacy by preventing images or document content from leaving the user's machine. This utility is significant for researchers and power users who need to digitize large, locked-down documents for AI-assisted analysis without relying on external cloud-based APIs.

Systems Hacking

Treating Executable Binaries as Queryable SQLite Databases

fzakaria.com  ·  105 pts  ·  14 comments

The experimental SELF format replaces traditional binary parsing with a relational database schema. By mapping symbol tables to SQL, developers gain atomic control over linking, patching, and binary auditing.

Read →Share →
[ Analysis + ]

Technical Analysis

"Your executable is a SQLite database" explores a radical proposal to replace the traditional ELF (Executable and Linkable Format) with a SQLite-based format called SELF (Structured Executable & Linkable Format). By mapping executable segments, symbol tables, and metadata into a SQL schema, the author demonstrates that common operations like linking, patching, and auditing can be performed through standard database queries and transactions. This approach replaces hand-rolled, complex binary parsing with a self-describing, extensible format that naturally supports features like atomic dependency management and cross-executable deduplication. The experiment highlights how modern database technologies can simplify low-level systems programming and offers a unique perspective on how OS-level tooling could evolve by treating binaries as queryable data.

Data Engineering

Building High-Performance Dashboards Using Static Parquet Files

hamiltonulmer.com  ·  19 pts  ·  0 comments

Eliminate the need for a dedicated backend by serving analytics directly from object storage. This approach uses strategically organized Parquet files and HTTP range requests to perform complex, drilldown queries in the browser.

Read →Share →
[ Analysis + ]

Technical Analysis

The author demonstrates how to build high-performance, drilldown analytics dashboards by leveraging a "data cube" approach using a single, strategically organized Parquet file stored on object storage like Cloudflare R2. By precomputing grouping sets and utilizing Parquet's metadata-rich structure, the client can use the `hyparquet` library to fetch only the necessary data segments via HTTP range requests, effectively eliminating the need for a dedicated backend database or query engine. This architecture shifts the computational complexity to the data pipeline, resulting in a lightweight, cost-effective solution for serving customer-facing analytics. The approach is significant because it highlights how modern browser-based readers can perform complex analytical queries on static files, providing a scalable and simplified alternative to traditional database-backed dashboards.

Game AI

Local AI Companion Brings Real-Time Agency to Skyrim

pantel.is  ·  177 pts  ·  36 comments

Experience a new level of interactive NPCs powered by local inference and action-latent encoding. This proof of concept demonstrates how low-latency AI can transcend scripted dialogue to become a true partner in open-world gaming.

Read →Share →
[ Analysis + ]

Technical Analysis

Developer pantelisk has created "Varkos," an AI gaming companion for *Skyrim* that prioritizes low-latency, real-time agency over simple dialogue, allowing it to perform complex, multi-step actions like fetching items or engaging in combat. The system utilizes a hybrid technical stack, combining traditional behavioral NLP, optimized local models like Qwen3-ASR, and a custom "Action Latent Encoder" to minimize response times to under 600ms on local hardware. By moving away from cloud-based LLM reliance toward local inference, the project aims to create a persistent, personality-driven experience that can evolve alongside the player. This work is significant because it shifts the focus of AI in gaming from scripted, reactive dialogue to proactive, agentic participation, offering a potential blueprint for more immersive, responsive NPCs in future titles.
