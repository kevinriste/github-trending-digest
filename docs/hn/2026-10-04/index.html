The HN Take

Video game history.

There are two ways to preserve video game history. One is to preserve the games. The other is to preserve the magazines that explained why the games you had not bought yet were going to change your life.

The Video Game History Foundation has now added more than 5,000 magazines to its digital archive:

Our magazine library now spans 46 years of video game history, from 1981-2026. Major new additions since launch include hundreds of trade magazines, a historically significant newsletter, and publications for niche gaming communities.

The collection totals nearly 600,000 pages, or "nearly 100 feet of shelf space," except that it is text-searchable and does not require 100 feet of shelf space. This is the basic archival trade: You scan enough paper to eliminate the physical library, then build a digital library to reproduce all of its organizational problems more efficiently.

It seems to be working. The foundation says its archive has already become Wikipedia's preferred reference source for out-of-print game magazines, with more than 500 citations. This is perhaps the final stage in the institutionalization of video game history. First you play Neo Geo games. Then you scan Neo Geo Freak. Then someone cites the scan on Wikipedia. Eventually a graduate student writes a dissertation about the scan's page layout.

The archive is also adding Japanese-language magazines, starting with the complete run of Neo Geo Freak. This required Japanese script recognition, right-to-left page layouts, search results without interword spacing and English indexing of major articles. It did not require translating all 10,000 pages; users can do that with their own tools, "even just using their phone camera."

This is sensible. The archive's job is to preserve and organize the historical record. Your phone's job is to hallucinate what it says.

Use the platform.

A standard piece of advice for web developers is "use the platform." If the browser already knows how to make a dialog box, you should use its dialog box rather than spending three days building your own worse dialog box in JavaScript.

This advice is obviously correct and psychologically impossible. Building your own dialog box is fun. You start with some positioned content, discover that the background scrolls, disable scrolling, add Escape-key handling, invent a focus trap, lose focus somewhere, add animations and eventually publish an npm package. Or you write "<dialog>" and go home. One approach teaches you a great deal about web development. The other works.

Nolan Lawson considers why developers keep reinventing things the platform already does. Some of it is history: Browsers used to lack important features, and libraries filled the gaps. Some of it is familiarity and documentation. And some of it is simply that "building things yourself is just more fun."

There is also an AI angle. The optimistic case is that coding agents have encyclopedic knowledge of platform APIs and will choose the smallest, fastest standard solution. The pessimistic case is that they "love duplicating code" and will generate endless custom machinery instead of noticing the helper function, CSS property or browser feature that already solves the problem.

Both seem plausible. The agent has read the entire web platform specification, but it has also read every Stack Overflow answer written before the relevant feature existed. It can either replace your baroque tangled mess with one line or generate the baroque tangled mess in four seconds. Progress!

The Game Archive Reaches 5,000 Magazines

gamehistory.org  ·  24 pts  ·  2 comments

The Video Game History Foundation has made more than 5,000 magazines searchable—nearly 600,000 pages spanning four decades of game culture. Its next chapter turns east, adding Japanese-language scans, OCR, and volunteer-built indexes for researchers.

Read →Share →
[ Analysis + ]

Technical Analysis

The Video Game History Foundation’s free digital archive has grown from 1,500 to more than 5,000 searchable video-game magazines, covering nearly 600,000 pages from 1981–2026, with most scans contributed by community and industry partners. The collection has already become a major research resource, including more than 500 citations on Wikipedia, while representing about 60% of the Foundation’s cataloged physical magazine holdings. Its next phase adds Japanese-language materials, beginning with the 1995–2000 magazine *Neo Geo Freak*, using Japanese OCR, language filtering, right-to-left layouts, and volunteer-created English indexes of significant articles. This expansion is important because Japanese magazines preserve hard-to-find developer interviews and firsthand gaming culture, while the archive’s technical and collaborative approach could make additional international collections more accessible for researchers.

Technical Analysis

The article challenges the popular claim that skilled trades are universally lucrative, accessible, physically safe, and protected from AI. It argues that college graduates still generally earn more, while the best-paid union and specialized trade positions are highly competitive and require training, credentials, and connections; most blue-collar workers do not have easy access to them. Trades also involve substantial injury risks and long-term physical costs, and technological progress—from pneumatic nailers to AI-assisted tools—may reduce demand for manual and even technical labor rather than merely replacing office work. The author concludes that trades can be rewarding for people suited to their physical demands, work styles, and culture, but career advice should distinguish among occupations and honestly weigh compensation, accessibility, safety, and automation.

France Blocks Access to Rodin’s Digital Doubles

cosmowenman.substack.com  ·  211 pts  ·  92 comments

After eight years of litigation, France’s highest administrative court ruled that publicly funded 3D scans of Rodin sculptures need not be released under freedom-of-information law. The decision leaves a public-domain collection behind an institutional gate, with the activist who sought access ordered to pay €3,000.

Read →Share →
[ Analysis + ]

Technical Analysis

Open-access activist Cosmo Wenman recounts an eight-year French freedom-of-information case seeking publicly funded 3D scans of Rodin’s public-domain sculptures. The scans—particularly laser-generated point-cloud files used routinely in cultural-heritage digitization—were initially deemed administrative documents that should be released, but the Rodin Museum resisted, citing alleged technical limitations, counterfeiting risks, trade secrecy, and commercial concerns. France’s Conseil d’État ultimately overturned the favorable lower-court outcome, ruling that the scans were effectively reproductions held as part of the museum’s inalienable collection and therefore outside FOI law, while ordering Wenman to pay €3,000. The decision matters because it may let publicly funded cultural institutions restrict access to digital representations of public-domain works, limiting research, preservation, reuse, and potential commercial innovation.

Agents Need Documents, Not Vague Memories

liao.gg  ·  190 pts  ·  107 comments

Conversation snippets and vector retrieval can preserve context without preserving understanding. A document-based workspace—plain Markdown, explicit decisions, versioned instructions—offers agents something quieter and more auditable: a shared record of how the work is supposed to proceed.

Read →Share →
[ Analysis + ]

Technical Analysis

The article argues that AI agents need structured, maintainable documentation rather than conventional “memory” plugins that extract conversation snippets, store embeddings, and retrieve similar fragments through RAG. It criticizes this approach as context-poor, difficult to audit, vulnerable to stale or incorrect information, and unable to surface knowledge the agent does not know to search for. Instead, it proposes a shared, document-based workspace containing instructions, specifications, decisions, research, and indexes that agents consult before working and update afterward. The author presents this model through the open-source Operator Memory plugin, emphasizing plain Markdown files that can be reviewed, versioned, and shared without vector databases or background summarization systems.
