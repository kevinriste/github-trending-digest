Invisible massive update.

Here is some reassuring release-note language from OBS Studio 33:

"This is a massive update to the underlying framework used for browser sources and docks from the July 2024 release to September 2026."

And:

"All of this work is on the backend and should be invisible to users. Please be sure to report any new weirdness or problems you run into with browser sources!"

Sure. The ideal software update is massive, entirely invisible and detectable only through new weirdness. If everything works, the developers successfully replaced more than two years of underlying browser infrastructure without you noticing. If it doesn't, congratulations, you are now part of the testing infrastructure.

OBS also completely overhauled plugin loading. First-party plugins move into a folder called "core," third-party plugins get a new installation structure, and old third-party plugins will keep loading from legacy locations until OBS Studio 34. This is the traditional compromise in software modernization: Everything has moved, but nothing has moved yet, and the next release is where the consequences live.

There are also improvements to number-entry boxes. "Typing is no longer mysteriously blocked in some situations," numbers are no longer mysteriously cut off, and pressing Enter now updates the value without closing the window. The massive invisible backend transformation is impressive, but allowing people to type numbers into number boxes is the sort of technological progress they can actually see.

Controlled cyber capability.

Okay here is an AI safety result:

On Kimi's in-house vulnerability benchmark, a version of K3.1 Pro trained without cyber restrictions solved 90.5% of the problems. The final model, after Kimi removed cyber-related material from both mid-training data and reinforcement-learning environments, solved 81.0%.

The benchmark gives the model vulnerable source code, a short bug hint and the corresponding binary, then asks it to develop an exploit, achieve arbitrary code execution in a separate sandbox and retrieve a secret string. So the safety intervention worked: It reduced performance from exploiting roughly nine out of 10 curated vulnerabilities to exploiting roughly four out of five.

The problem is that cyber capability is apparently not a neat detachable module labeled CYBER. Kimi removed cyber-specific data while retaining general coding tasks, but stronger coding and long-horizon debugging abilities still generalized back into vulnerability research. You can teach the model less hacking, but if you teach it more programming it might figure hacking out again. Computers are difficult that way.

Kimi's principle is that it will not release open weights that "extend the absolute frontier of cyber capability." K3.1 Pro remains behind frontier proprietary models that have been publicly available for at least three months, so its full weights will be released by Nov. 4. This is a coherent safety standard: An open model does not have to be bad at hacking; it just has to be somewhat worse than the best closed model that people could already use.

Kimi also says it intends to "progressively raise the cyber capability available in open weights," on the theory that broadly diffusing dual-use capability will let more teams audit their own software and ultimately improve security. Again, coherent. Today's safe open model can exploit 81% of the benchmark. Tomorrow's safe open model can exploit more, once proprietary models have moved farther ahead. AI safety is a moving walkway, and everyone is walking in the same direction.

Technical Analysis

Douglas Ross’s “I Don’t Understand Quantum Physics” is an accessible, broad introduction to quantum mechanics, moving from classical physics and landmark experiments to wave-particle duality, matter waves, uncertainty, wavefunctions, tunneling, superposition, and competing interpretations. It explains how quantum theory models particles and fields probabilistically rather than through the deterministic trajectories of classical physics, while also addressing measurement, collapse, hidden variables, and thought experiments such as Schrödinger’s cat and EPR. The article includes a glossary, formulas, and references, making it useful as a conceptual guide rather than a specialized research paper or business analysis. Its importance lies in clarifying the counterintuitive foundations of a theory underlying technologies such as semiconductors, lasers, and quantum computing.

Pure Functional Code Closes on C

rtfeldman.com  ·  132 pts  ·  19 comments

Roc’s DEFLATE compressor comes within 8.9 percent of libdeflate on one AMD system and 2.6 percent on an M1. The benchmark is narrow, but its safety and purity guarantees make the result worth measuring.

[ Analysis + ]

Technical Analysis

The article evaluates how close Roc, a pure functional programming language, can get to highly optimized C by comparing its DEFLATE compressor with the SIMD-heavy libdeflate library. After extensive optimization, roc-deflate was a median 8.9% slower on an AMD Ryzen system and 2.6% slower on an M1 Mac, though performance varied by input and occasionally exceeded libdeflate. The result is notable because Roc retains enforced memory safety, purity, deterministic behavior, automatic memory management, and stronger supply-chain restrictions, while lacking unsafe escape hatches and arbitrary FFI. It demonstrates that these guarantees can coexist with near-C performance in at least one demanding workload, while acknowledging that C still has a higher optimization ceiling and that broader benchmarks are needed to assess how generalizable the result is.
