The HN Take

Eight bits of trust

Oh sure:

"One of eMudhra's certificate authority private keys is the number 4."

Not a number beginning with 4, followed by enough random digits to secure global communications. Four. Another private key was 56. Eight intermediate certificate keys, all created in April 2025, were numbers between 4 and 237.

These were elliptic-curve keys on the P-384 curve. The normal idea is that the private key is an enormous random number, so deriving it from the public certificate is impractical. Here the keys had 47 bytes of zeros followed by one byte. There were at most 255 possibilities. A laptop could try all of them in less than a second.

"So the whole thing, every certificate those CAs could ever issue, every client that trusted them, rested on eight bits."

Right. The global certificate authority system is a large formal hierarchy of cryptographic trust, implemented here by choosing a number from roughly the same range as a restaurant coat-check ticket.

This is worse than an ordinary stolen key. With a stolen key, there is a thief and a theft date, and investigators can ask who had access after that date. These keys were effectively public from the moment the certificates were created. Anyone who saw the certificates could recover them, and there is no way to know whether anyone did during the 17 months before the problem was reported.

The keys could be used to issue apparently valid certificates for any website or service. Chrome, Firefox, Apple and Microsoft have blocked the affected certificates, and Certificate Transparency records indicate that major browsers could not have been attacked through them. Good.

The rest of the software ecosystem is less reassuring. Curl, Python, Node, Go, Java, Git, package managers, CI systems, embedded devices and assorted scripts often trust a certificate bundle on disk while checking neither revocation nor Certificate Transparency. The researcher generated a certificate for api.stripe.com and found that default curl reported "SSL certificate verify ok," Python completed the handshake and Node said "authorized: true."

The fix for devices that can be updated is to update them. The fix for routers, appliances and industrial equipment whose certificate bundles are frozen until 2039 is to travel back to April 2025 and suggest choosing a private key larger than 4.

By the way, eMudhra says a script bug created a second weak key pair on its hardware security module, and human error caused the wrong keys to be used. The researcher also reports that Claude recovered the private keys with a single prompt. Artificial intelligence has finally cracked public-key cryptography, in the narrow sense that someone made the private key 4.

The point of return

The United Nations Environment Programme says the Paris Agreement's goal of limiting global warming to 1.5 degrees Celsius by 2100 is no longer achievable without overshooting it.

"The opportunity to bridge the 1.5 degrees Celsius emissions gap with 'no or limited' overshoot has been lost."

The new plan is to keep warming below 2 degrees, then remove enough carbon dioxide from the atmosphere to return to 1.5 degrees by the end of the century. So 1.5 degrees remains the destination, but it is no longer available by the direct route.

This would require global greenhouse gas emissions to fall 18 percent by 2030, 45 percent by 2035 and 69 percent by 2040 relative to 2025. Current government commitments, even if fully implemented, would cut emissions only 5 to 8 percent by 2030 and 11 to 15 percent by 2035.

Then comes the cleanup. UNEP estimates that humanity would need to remove 320 gigatons of carbon dioxide by the end of the century, equivalent to eight times the emissions recorded in 2024, using reforestation and technologies involving compression, mineralisation and underground storage.

The original plan was to stop putting so much carbon into the atmosphere. The revised plan is to keep putting too much carbon into the atmosphere for a while, stop before reaching 2 degrees, and then take 320 gigatons back out. We have moved from climate prevention to climate returns processing.

1. Eight Bits of Trust; eMudhra's CA keys cracked in milliseconds

scotthelme.co.uk | hkc | 133 points | 54 comments | discussion

First seen: October 21, 2026 | Consecutive daily streak: 1 day

Analysis

The story reports that eMudhra issued eight TLS and code-signing intermediate CA certificates whose P-384 private keys were accidentally tiny values—single-byte numbers such as 4, 56, and 115—making them recoverable from public certificates in milliseconds. Because anyone could have derived these keys since the certificates were created, attackers could potentially forge certificates for arbitrary websites or sign malware, with risk extending beyond browsers to command-line tools, CI/CD systems, APIs, and unpatchable IoT devices that do not check revocation or Certificate Transparency. Major browsers blocked the affected certificates and roots, but many other clients remain vulnerable until their CA bundles are updated. The incident appears to have resulted from a key-generation or certificate-signing workflow error, underscoring how a basic randomness or process failure can undermine an entire certificate authority’s trust chain.

5. Whatever Happened to Fire Escapes

practical.engineering | sohkamyung | 12 points | 1 comments | discussion

First seen: October 21, 2026 | Consecutive daily streak: 1 day

Analysis

The story traces the rise and decline of external fire escapes in American cities, from their adoption as inexpensive retrofits for dangerous 19th-century tenements to their replacement by enclosed, fire-resistant interior stairways. It explains that exterior escapes were often poorly maintained, obstructed, difficult to use under panic, vulnerable to structural failure, and sometimes created security or accessibility problems, while disasters such as the Triangle Shirtwaist fire exposed their limitations. Building codes gradually shifted toward safer daily-use stairs, supported by improvements in fire-rated materials, sprinklers, alarms, and smoke protection, though older escapes often remain. The broader lesson is that building regulations balance safety, cost, usability, and limited space, and that seemingly narrow code decisions can create long-term, unintended effects on construction and affordability.

8. The Decoherence Trap

maevyn.codes | cassianoleal | 54 points | 14 comments | discussion

Analysis

“The Decoherence Trap” argues that complex systems accumulate “decoherence” when incremental changes introduce redundant, incompatible, or poorly understood models, making future modifications increasingly costly and risky. Using plumbing, software architecture, and a graph-based simulation as examples, the essay contends that rushed development and overlapping AI-generated changes can create runaway complexity: more code and faster output without more completed work. This matters commercially because review, maintenance, and feature delivery can slow sharply as organizations inherit systems that require broader expertise and careful translation between paradigms. The author also challenges rapid AI takeoff narratives, suggesting that agents may worsen the coherence problem and face practical limits in improving software without reliable feedback from the real world.
