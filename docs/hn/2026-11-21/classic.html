The HN Take

GRH

The Riemann Hypothesis has been open for about 150 years, but apparently Friday was urgent.

First, Ravi Vakil interrupted an American Mathematical Society meeting to announce that Alessandro Maria Masullo had a proposed proof of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis:

"It is all human, with no AI."

Brian Conrad had been reviewing it for months, the plan was to spend another year polishing the writing and circulating it privately, but "now it seems best to put out the current draft." Eight hours later, Vakil added an update:

"Several people have contacted me to report problems in the proposed proof, identified with the assistance of different AI models."

To be clear, reported problems are not necessarily fatal, and Vakil makes no claim about whether the proof strategy is correct. But there is something wonderfully efficient about the new peer-review system. A human spends years proving GRH, another human spends months checking it, and then several AIs find possible problems before the first human gets out of his meetings.

Meanwhile Levent Alpoge announced a separate proof of GRH that he says he discovered with Claude. It is formalized in Lean, but the human-readable explanation is not finished. In fact the formal proof is apparently more than 250,000 pages, and Alpoge writes that he is "presenting the world with a complete argument before having mastered a massive neighbourhood around its mathematics."

So we have two proposed GRH proofs. The human proof is being checked by AI, while the AI proof is being checked by humans. The human proof was supposed to be polished for a year but was released immediately; the AI proof is too large to read but is already formalized. Eventually one imagines the optimal workflow will be for the AI to write the proof, another AI to find the problems, Lean to verify it, and mathematicians to receive an urgent announcement explaining that there is a long story to tell later.

Internal monologue

Thinking Machines released Inkling 2 Mini, a 26.4 billion parameter open-weights model that fits in about 20 gigabytes of memory and can run on a consumer laptop. Its main architectural novelty is that it runs its middle layers three times for every token:

"Looping allows the model to reason longer without writing its reasoning down, which matters for monitoring."

Yes. Traditionally the comforting theory of chain-of-thought monitoring is that the model writes down what it is thinking, so humans can inspect the reasoning and notice if it is plotting something bad. Inkling can instead think more without writing it down. This matters for monitoring in approximately the way that encrypted messaging matters for wiretapping.

Still, the results were reassuring. As the number of loops increased, the model's written chain of thought became shorter but "stayed legible throughout," and it did not trend higher on tests of agentic sabotage. A research variant also kept improving on serial reasoning out to 10 loops, twice as many as it was trained with. Apparently you can give the model more time to think privately and it becomes better at reasoning without becoming measurably more treacherous. Good employee, fewer status updates.

The model also ran offline on a MacBook Pro during a flight and built a playable flight simulator in a few minutes. This is useful because commercial aviation has historically involved an awkward separation between the people using flight simulators and the people currently on flights.

3. Inkling 2 Mini

thinkingmachines.ai | jasondavies | 1060 points | 198 comments | discussion

First seen: November 21, 2026 | Consecutive daily streak: 1 day

Analysis

Thinking Machines released Inkling 2 Mini, an open-weight 26.4B-parameter Mixture-of-Experts model with 3.4B active parameters that uses looping—repeating its middle layers—to increase reasoning depth without generating as many chain-of-thought tokens. Designed for agentic coding, search, and tool use, it reportedly leads similarly sized open models on many benchmarks while using about 20GB of memory, making it practical for consumer laptops and other local hardware. The release also includes a variable-loop research model and experiments suggesting that looping improves no-CoT and long-horizon reasoning, shortens but does not obviously obscure written reasoning, and does not increase measured sabotage tendencies at this scale. Its limitations include weaker factual knowledge than larger models, poorer multi-constraint and multi-turn performance, and lower scores on some advanced reasoning tasks; the models are available with open weights and through Tinker.

4. RCE in Gnupg

lists.gnupg.org | gdgghhhhh | 41 points | 3 comments | discussion

First seen: November 21, 2026 | Consecutive daily streak: 1 day

Analysis

GnuPG has released GPGME 2.3.1 to fix a critical double-free vulnerability, CVE-2026-129975 (T8557), triggered by an OpenPGP key containing an unusually long user ID. A stale pointer left after a buffer reallocation can cause a second free, potentially allowing data exfiltration and possibly remote code execution, though RCE has not been confirmed. The flaw affects applications using older GPGME versions, including Gpg4win, GnuPG Desktop, mail and XMPP clients, Samba, and statically linked software. Users and distributors should update the shared library or rebuild statically linked binaries against GPGME 2.3.1, with updated Gpg4win and related desktop releases also available.

5. ClawDOS – An AI coding agent for DOS

ricapar.net | Ricapar | 84 points | 22 comments | discussion

First seen: November 21, 2026 | Consecutive daily streak: 1 day

Analysis

ClawDOS is an open-source AI coding agent that runs natively on an IBM PC XT with an 8088 processor, 640 KB of RAM, CGA graphics, and DOS, allowing users to chat with Claude, manipulate files, compile and execute programs, inspect screenshots, and retain conversation memories. Its roughly 176 KB harness implements the API client, streamed responses, tools, UI, and local history on the vintage machine, while a separate modern computer provides a TLS tunnel because the XT cannot handle HTTPS. The project demonstrates that contemporary agent workflows can operate under extreme hardware and software constraints, including producing a playable WarGames-inspired demo on the PC itself. It is primarily an engineering and nostalgia experiment rather than a commercial product, released under the GPL with setup support for emulators and real hardware.
