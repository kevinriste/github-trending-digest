The HN Take

Office-hours scraper.

Here is one approach to AI data sourcing:

On Oct. 15 and 16, a crawler identifying itself as "trellis2-data-sourcing/1.0 (3D foundation model research; [redacted]@roblox.com)" read almost the entire catalog of Open3DLab, SFMLab and SmutBase. It then downloaded 1,027 files from 712 projects, totaling 145.5 gigabytes.

Open3DLab says the crawler ignored its robots.txt file, terms of service and creators' licenses. It also downloaded adult content indiscriminately: 345 of the 712 projects were adult projects, including 224 from SmutBase, which hosts adult content only. Roblox did not respond to two requests for comment, and Open3DLab cannot prove that the files were collected for AI training or used in any model.

Still, the crawler said "3D foundation model research," its contact address belonged to a researcher who builds 3D foundation models at Roblox, and Roblox has been building a 3D foundation model. There are mysteries in life, but this is perhaps not the hardest one.

The funny detail is that the crawler was ethically unconstrained but professionally well-adjusted. After Open3DLab blocked it, it kept trying to scrape, but its activity "seemed to fit neatly within office hours," briefly pausing around lunchtime and stopping near the end of the workday in Central European time. It ignored copyright licenses, contractual restrictions, robots.txt and the distinction between children's game assets and explicit adult models, but it took lunch. Work-life balance is important.

Roblox says that more than a third of its daily users were under 13 in 2025, and its platform bans sexual content. Presumably any generative product would have safety filters. But there are two possible approaches to building a child-safe 3D model. One is to filter the training data before downloading it. The other is to bulk-download an adult model repository and then build a sufficiently good system for preventing the resulting model from showing children what it learned. The second approach creates more jobs for AI safety researchers.

Also, AI companies sometimes defend large-scale data collection on the theory that publicly accessible data is available to be read and learned from. There is an intuitive analogy to a person browsing a website for inspiration. Here the person would have browsed 18,926 project pages in 34 hours, downloaded 145.5 gigabytes of files, ignored every posted instruction and taken a civilized lunch break. A very fast, very rude person with excellent boundaries around working hours.

Raw research data.

Okay!

OpenAI announced three AI-generated results in mathematics, computer science and physics. It says its models proved the local smoothing conjecture in every dimension, which would also resolve the Kakeya and Stein restriction conjectures in all dimensions. It says a model disproved the Exponential Time Hypothesis, a foundational conjecture used in hundreds of computer science papers. And it says its models produced mathematically rigorous existence proofs for certain four-dimensional quantum field theories, including Quantum Chromodynamics, with a subproblem covering the existence portion of the Yang-Mills Millennium Prize problem.

Those would be very large results. OpenAI has Lean formalizations for the mathematics and computer science claims, and says it completed a Lean formalization of Yang-Mills existence. But a formal proof artifact is not the same thing as broad scientific understanding or community acceptance, so OpenAI has developed an interesting new research workflow: The AI produces the discoveries, and groups of eminent human scientists are recruited to figure out what happened.

OpenAI writes:

"In our view, these manuscripts are best understood as a form of raw research data."

Traditionally, raw research data was the stuff you collected before doing the research. Now the raw data is a purported solution to a decades-old conjecture, and the research consists of reverse-engineering why the solution works and what it means. Scientific discovery has become the input to scientific understanding.

The three results are getting three different release strategies. The math output and Lean proof are public, and researchers can organize themselves. The computer science materials are public, with a group led by Russell Impagliazzo, Ramamohan Paturi and Ryan Williams asked to explain and verify them. The physics group, led by David Gross and including Michael Douglas, Arthur Jaffe, Igor Klebanov and Nikita Nekrasov, will hold the AI artifacts while it works on a writeup.

OpenAI emphasizes four principles for these collaborations: transparency, no academic credit for OpenAI, recognition for the humans' explanatory work, and independence. Participation is "voluntary and unpaid." OpenAI will not claim academic credit for making the discoveries; the researchers will receive recognition for understanding and writing them up; and they will not be paid for participating. OpenAI does say it will fund a conference about the ETH result and points to a $5 million contribution to the Simons Foundation for workshops and related programs.

There is a coherent division of labor here. The AI gets the theorem. OpenAI gets to announce that its AI got the theorem. Academics get recognition for checking, contextualizing and explaining the theorem. Nobody gets academic credit for discovering the theorem, because the thing that discovered it is software and the company operating the software does not want credit. The discovery simply appeared, like a meteorite, except with a corporate communications page.

Perhaps this is the future of research. Models will produce more purported breakthroughs than human experts can quickly absorb, and the scarce academic skill will no longer be proving theorems but deciding which machine-generated proofs are important, correct and comprehensible. The prestige hierarchy will move from "I solved the problem" to "I was selected to shepherd the folder containing the solution." The AI does science; the humans do due diligence.

The article revisits Brooks’s Law—that adding people to a late software project can make it later—in the context of AI coding agents and LLMs. Although agents avoid human onboarding and coordination costs, they do not eliminate sequential dependencies, ambiguous requirements, code review, or integration work.
