The HN Take

Nvidia options.

Here is a long-term investing strategy:

Eric Gullichsen says he was an early technical adviser to Nvidia in 1993, when Jensen Huang did not yet wear the leather jacket, and received options on 25,000 shares. In 1996, Nvidia's chief financial officer told him that 15,625 shares had vested and that he needed to exercise them. He did, then "forgot all about it."

This turned out to be an expensive thing to forget about.

Gullichsen says the signed option agreement provided for all 25,000 shares to vest within one year, while Nvidia and its outside counsel treated the grant as vesting over four years. The difference was 9,375 old Nvidia shares. After a cumulative 480-for-1 in stock splits, that would be 4.5 million current shares, worth roughly the billion dollars in his headline.

Thirty years later, he found the paperwork in a folder while Nvidia was busy becoming, in his words, "the Most Important Company on Earth." He hired lawyers. Nvidia, he says, did not dispute the authenticity of the agreement, but argued that the claim was time-barred. Its lawyers' eventual answer was, in essence, "so sue us." His own lawyers concluded that the statute of limitations probably prevented him from getting past a motion to dismiss.

This is in some sense the ultimate Nvidia investment story. He got in before almost everyone, held for more than 30 years, ignored every market fluctuation, never panic-sold, benefited from every stock split and ended up with approximately zero dollars. Diamond hands, but on the wrong number of shares.

There is a conventional lesson here about carefully checking your equity documents. But that advice undersells the difficulty. In 1996, the disputed 9,375 shares were options in a struggling chip company whose first product had been badly hurt by Microsoft's decision not to support quadratic texture mapping in DirectX. To realize that a vesting discrepancy was worth fighting over, Gullichsen would have needed not only to read the contract carefully but also to forecast the future of computer graphics, gaming, cryptocurrency and artificial intelligence. Perhaps the contract said one year, but the trade required a 30-year model.

The statute of limitations is also a sort of anti-compounding mechanism. Nvidia's stock can split and appreciate for decades, turning a small administrative disagreement into a billion-dollar claim. The legal right, meanwhile, apparently depreciates to zero while sitting in a folder. Time in the market beats timing the market, except for litigation, where apparently you should check the folder.

Thinking less.

Artificial intelligence models are increasingly valuable because of how much they think, and increasingly expensive because they won't stop thinking. Fireworks AI has a product for that:

"Ember-1 is a new specialized model from Fireworks Research that delivers Kimi K3's quality with 40% fewer tokens."

The problem is that reasoning models can spend "sometimes more than 90%" of their generated tokens on internal reasoning rather than the answer. In a multi-turn agent workflow, all that reasoning can be fed back into later calls, so an early bout of contemplation gets reread and rebilled repeatedly. The model thinks, then thinks about its prior thinking, then charges you to remember having thought.

Fireworks says it ran more than 50 training experiments and 200 evaluations to teach Ember-1 to preserve useful reflection while cutting "unnecessary reasoning and escaping unproductive loops." Across seven benchmarks and two customers' production traffic, it says Kimi K3's reasoning could be shortened by 35% to 50% without sacrificing accuracy. In an internal rollout, Fireworks' developers did not notice the switch.

"The outcome we're proudest of: no news."

Sure. The highest compliment you can pay an enterprise AI system is that nobody noticed it had become dramatically less introspective. It did the same work, emitted fewer private monologues and cost less. This is also approximately the performance review that many companies would like to give their human employees.

There is an appealing progression here. First the industry trained models to reason at length because longer reasoning improved answers. Then customers received the bill. Now the industry is training models to determine which parts of their reasoning were unnecessary. Eventually perhaps the model will develop the ultimate form of metacognition: looking at a task, estimating the token economics and deciding not to have so many thoughts about it.

3. Footguns with Postgres "at time zone 'UTC'"

bookofrevenue.com | birdculture | 36 points | 23 comments | discussion

First seen: September 28, 2026 | Consecutive daily streak: 1 day

Analysis

The article explains a PostgreSQL footgun involving `AT TIME ZONE 'UTC'`, which converts a `timestamptz` (an absolute instant) into a timezone-less `timestamp` rather than merely normalizing its display. This matters when manipulating time-series data, such as adding calendar months for UTC-based reporting, because month arithmetic depends on the session timezone and can produce incorrect dates. The resulting `timestamp` can also behave unexpectedly in comparisons with `timestamptz`, even when the displayed times appear equivalent. To perform UTC-based arithmetic safely while preserving correct equality semantics, the query may need to apply `AT TIME ZONE 'UTC'` before the calculation and again afterward to convert the value back to `timestamptz`.

Comment Analysis

The discussion focused mainly on PostgreSQL’s time types, conversions, and comparison behavior. Commenters differed on whether the resulting hazards call for clearer semantics, application-level handling of business rules, or more careful use of the existing behavior; smaller threads considered the limits of generic month arithmetic.

Time types and naming are confusing — Commenters distinguished timestamps without a time zone from absolute instants, and noted that the names can obscure what each type stores.

The type `timestamp` is not a timestamp at all because it doesn't encode a unique point in time, it just stores a date and a time which has to be interpreted relative to a timezone.ulrikrasmussen
All `timestamptz` is is UNIX-style Epoch timestamp, that is an absolute point in time in UTC.globular-toast

A time zone is not simply part of an instant — A technical correction pointed out that a zoned date-time can be ambiguous or nonexistent, and that future offsets can change, so it cannot always be treated as an instant paired with a time zone.

zoned datetimes carry ambiguities as to their actual location on the timeline (because they can repeat, or not exist at all)masklinn

Mixed-type comparisons depend on the session time zone — A commenter challenged the claim that comparing a timestamp with a timestamptz always returns false, explaining that PostgreSQL interprets the timestamp using the session time zone. The commenter also pointed to an alternative available in PostgreSQL 16.

4. Thinking fast and slow in AI: The role of metacognition (2021)

arxiv.org | teleforce | 110 points | 36 comments | discussion

First seen: September 28, 2026 | Consecutive daily streak: 1 day

Analysis

The paper proposes SOFAI, a multi-agent architecture inspired by Kahneman’s “fast” and “slow” thinking, to improve AI systems’ adaptability, robustness, and decision quality. A fast System 1 solver uses past experience to produce an immediate, constant-time answer, while a slower System 2 solver performs more expensive reasoning when needed. A centralized metacognitive module monitors solver confidence, expected rewards, resource availability, past performance, and environmental information, then decides whether the fast answer is sufficient or the slow solver’s added cost is justified. Its two-stage assessment is intended to conserve time and compute while reserving deliberation for difficult or high-value decisions. The authors outline planned evaluations in constrained pathfinding and epistemic planning, but present the architecture largely as ongoing research rather than a validated production system.

Comment Analysis

The discussion centers on whether fast/slow thinking offers a useful model for AI reasoning, especially whether adaptive reasoning in a single model is equivalent to distinct reasoning systems coordinated by a metacognitive choice. Other comments question how closely human cognition maps to AI and whether cognition can be usefully divided into neat modules.
