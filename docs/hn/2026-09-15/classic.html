The HN Take

Shell inflation.

Here is a monetary policy story:

In 1930, Australian gold prospectors reached the highlands of New Guinea and encountered roughly a million people whom the colonial governments and coastal populations apparently did not know were there. The highlanders had little knowledge of the outside world and initially thought the Australians might be ghosts. They investigated this theory by watching them defecate and smelling the result, which established that they were human. Due diligence was more tactile then.

The Australians brought cameras, records, mirrors, dolls and airplanes. But the important financial technology was shells. Shells were extremely rare in the highlands, functioned roughly as currency and status goods, and could make their owner rich and powerful. The Australians understood this and brought them in by plane:

"At first this felt like a great deal for the highlanders, who were willing to give up nearly everything for more shells. But the planes kept coming, and the price of everything in shells kept rising, until it went from one shell making someone a leader and 'rich', to almost all people, including children, being draped in hundreds of shells, and the shell eventually being worthless."

Sure. If you discover a society whose money is difficult to obtain because it has to travel through many hostile valleys from the coast, and you have airplanes, you have discovered seigniorage. You can print their money by flying it in. You exchange newly imported shells for food and labor, the money supply expands, prices rise, everyone becomes nominally rich, and eventually children are walking around wearing hundreds of units of the former reserve currency as jewelry.

This is approximately the standard story of hyperinflation, except that the money printer has wings and the inflation ends with everyone looking fabulous.

Slop audit.

Here is one way to measure how much open-source software is written by artificial intelligence:

You can't.

Still, one F-Droid maintainer inspected the repositories behind 102 apps updated on Sept. 12 and classified them based on recent commits, agent infrastructure, AI disclosures, README aesthetics, icons and general vibes. The author is admirably direct about the methodology:

"There's no way to effectively detect slop."

And:

"I did not build a 'slop detector' or anything like that."

Instead the ratings are based on "looking at recent commits and their content along with the project's branding." Repositories with Claude co-authorship, agent files or suspiciously polished planning documents tend to land in "Mostly AI." Repositories where nothing looks suspicious land in "No signs of AI." One project gets "???" because its owner appears to copy changes manually through GitHub's web editor, which is apparently now evidence of humanity.

This is not science, but it is arguably the correct form of AI detection. The author's theory is that the point of coding agents is to let developers be lazy, and that this laziness leaks into the surrounding repository. Why write the README yourself? Why review the code yourself? Why remove the planning files? "It's low effort all the way down."

There is a nice circularity here. You cannot reliably identify AI-written code from the code, so you identify it from the developer's refusal to clean up after the AI. The robot leaves fingerprints because the human could not be bothered to wipe them off. Eventually the agents will learn to produce tasteful commit messages, restrained READMEs and plausible signs of human frustration. Then the most reliable proof that software was written by a person will be that the repository is confusing and badly maintained.

1. I can't stop thinking about Papua New Guinea

notnottalmud.substack.com | networked | 262 points | 81 comments | discussion

First seen: September 15, 2026 | Consecutive daily streak: 1 day

Analysis

The article explores the fascinating history of Papua New Guinea’s highlands, which remained isolated from the outside world for roughly 10,000 years until a surprising 1930 expedition revealed a massive, flourishing society. The author highlights how, despite the independent invention of agriculture, the region’s difficult geography and lack of pack animals or durable storage prevented the centralization seen in other civilizations, leading to a persistent tribal structure that still shapes the nation today. While commodities like sweet potatoes and sea shells slowly infiltrated the interior through trade, advanced technology and ideas failed to traverse the rugged, malaria-prone mountain barriers. This story is significant because it documents one of the last major "first contact" events in human history, offering a rare, film-documented glimpse into a society that evolved along a completely divergent path from the rest of the world.

Analysis

By evaluating code repositories for "AI smells"—such as agentic infrastructure, LLM-authored READMEs, and co-authored commits—the author categorized nearly 73% of the sampled apps as predominantly AI-generated. This experiment highlights a growing trend in the FOSS community where "vibe-coding" is becoming common practice, raising concerns about the long-term maintainability, security, and quality of open-source software. The findings suggest that the reliance on automated coding tools has shifted the landscape of independent software development, presenting a significant dilemma for users who value the craftsmanship of human-authored code.

Author rmoff evaluates several options—including S3Proxy, SeaweedFS, and Garage—based on their ease of deployment via Docker, license transparency, and suitability for simple, single-node configurations. The article highlights that while projects like SeaweedFS and S3Proxy offer functional, lightweight replacements, others like Apache Ozone are too complex for local development needs. This transition is important because it underscores the fragility of relying on third-party infrastructure tools for development environments when those projects shift their commercial direction or governance models.
