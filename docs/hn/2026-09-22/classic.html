The HN Take

The beautiful ad

The University of Bristol used the UK's most powerful supercomputer to watch the entire FIFA World Cup and count the ads. This is perhaps not the most inspiring possible use of the UK's most powerful supercomputer, but it did find a lot of ads:

The team used the UK's most powerful supercomputer, Isambard-AI, based at University of Bristol, to analyse all 172.6 hours of live play across the tournament's 104 matches.

Harmful product branding appeared in every match and was visible during 39.3 hours of live play, almost a quarter of all live play. Viewers could not avoid this marketing without also missing some of the football.

Right. You can skip television commercials by leaving the room, but you cannot skip the pitchside ads without leaving during the football, which is traditionally the bit people watch. The advertising has been physically incorporated into the game. If you want to see whether the striker scores, you must also receive a brief visual update from a soft drink, fast-food, sports drink or crisps company.

Unhealthy food and drink accounted for 70.4% of the branding detected. Prediction markets appeared 9,360 times, alcohol 9,266 times, gambling 6,429 times and cryptocurrency 2,632 times. There is a coherent consumer journey here. You watch football, eat crisps, drink beer, bet on the next goal and buy crypto. If the bet and the crypto go badly, perhaps you drink another beer. A full commercial ecosystem.

The particularly modern part is that FIFA broadcast the same embedded ads into more than 220 territories, including places where some of the advertised products were restricted, illegal or offered by unlicensed operators. Technology exists to replace pitchside advertising for different countries, but FIFA did not use it for that purpose. The beautiful game brings the world together, including around products that much of the world has specifically decided not to advertise.

Random except for zero

Here is a possible problem with AMD's random-number generator:

Recently, while doing some assembly code to render data intro charts, and using 'rdrand' and 'rdseed' to provide that data, I've figured out by accident what appears to be a bug only with AMD processors: they can't generate a number 0 at a given size!

This is an unconfirmed report on an assembly-language forum, based on tests of two AMD processors, so perhaps the bug is in the test program or affects only particular chips. AMD reportedly escalated the case internally but had not provided a substantive answer.

Still, the reported result is charming. The program examines 16-bit outputs, for which zero should come up occasionally, and the author says that after days of testing AMD produced none. Intel produced zero normally. One commenter offered the obvious product-management theory:

"I hope this is not something stupid from AMD that decided zero is not a random number."

It would be an understandable philosophical mistake. If you ask a person for a random number, zero feels like refusing to participate. If you ask a processor, though, zero is one of the numbers. A random-number generator that produces every possible value except zero is almost random, in the same way that a roulette wheel that never lands on one particular number is almost fair. For many applications the difference is negligible. For cryptography, "almost random, except for the unexplained thing the hardware secretly never does" is not normally the specification you want.

2. AMD's random number generator can't generate a 0?

Analysis

A programmer using the `rdrand` and `rdseed` assembly instructions to render data discovered that AMD processors consistently fail to generate a zero, a behavior not observed on Intel chips. Using a custom diagnostic tool, the developer confirmed this anomaly across multiple AMD systems, suggesting a fundamental difference in how AMD implements its hardware random number generator. The finding sparked community debate regarding the reliability of hardware-accelerated entropy sources versus software-based alternatives and the persistent difficulty of verifying "black box" silicon logic. While the developer has escalated the issue to AMD, the incident highlights ongoing concerns among security-conscious developers regarding the transparency and potential implementation flaws of CPU-level cryptographic primitives.

Comment Analysis

The discussion was largely skeptical of the bug's practical severity while accepting that it reflected a genuine AMD implementation or instruction-width defect, particularly after commenters reproduced it on Zen systems. Participants debated whether RDRAND must be exactly uniform, whether CSPRNG seeding or modulo reduction mitigates the issue, and how such a defect could pass hardware validation. Alternative explanations involving sampling, distribution assumptions, or deliberate zero suppression received limited support.

3. Can gzip be a language model?

Analysis

This article explores the theoretical and practical equivalence between data compression and language modeling, demonstrating that the standard DEFLATE algorithm used by `gzip` can function as a rudimentary text generator. By leveraging the compressor's tendency to assign shorter codes to predictable sequences, the author implements a beam search method to score and select probable text continuations based on a provided corpus. While the output lacks the coherence of modern neural networks, the project serves as a compelling demonstration of how information theory principles allow compressors to act as implicit probability models. This experiment is significant because it highlights the fundamental relationship between compression efficiency and predictive performance without requiring specialized machine learning architecture.

Analysis

The article argues for replacing the term "watermark" with "spymark" when referring to hidden, imperceptible signals embedded in digital media that link content to a specific user's identity. Unlike traditional watermarks used for ownership or authenticity, these clandestine payloads—such as those generated by Google’s SynthID—can encode sensitive metadata like database IDs that survive file edits and compression. The author contends that these tracking mechanisms represent a significant privacy threat, as they are often implemented without user consent and can facilitate the involuntary surveillance of individuals. By adopting the term "spymark," the author aims to shift the discourse to emphasize that these technologies are designed for tracking rather than mere content identification.
