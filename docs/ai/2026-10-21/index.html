AI biologists

Anthropic has built a molecular biology lab for Claude:

"We're introducing a new life sciences research group and laboratory at Anthropic. Our focus is on fundamental biology research using Claude: exploring datasets of DNA to identify uncharacterized protein families, generating hypotheses at scale, and testing them through experiments in the lab."

Sure. Claude does the computational biology, human scientists do the lab work, and eventually everyone learns which job is harder to automate.

In one early project, Anthropic prompted Claude to search a large DNA database for unusual reverse transcriptases, enzymes that copy RNA into DNA. Roughly 950 agents spent 21 hours and 210 million tokens gathering more than 200,000 reverse transcriptases, identifying 3,500 candidate systems and narrowing those down to 20 worth examining. One agent noticed a reverse transcriptase next to an array of repeating DNA:

"[The DNA next to the RT] is spectacular: I can see by eye a tandem repeat array ... that's a CRISPR-like ... repeat array?!"

The machine scientist uses exclamation points when it sees interesting genomic architecture, which is reassuringly human, though perhaps the exclamation points were in the training data.

Anthropic calls the system array-associated reverse transcriptases, or ART. The underlying reverse transcriptase had appeared in prior research, but Claude seems to have been the first to connect it with the neighboring repeat array and an accessory protein of unknown function. Human scientists then confirmed that the array produces distinct short RNAs. That combination resembles a small number of known programmable biological systems that can cut, copy or paste DNA.

Does ART do any of those things? Nobody knows. Anthropic is explicit that its function remains unknown and experiments are continuing. So the discovery is not "Claude invented a new CRISPR." It is "Claude found a peculiar arrangement in bacteriophage DNA that looks enough like CRISPR to justify putting scientists in a lab." That is less cinematic, but it is fairly close to what biological discovery normally looks like.

The traditional model is that a scientist spends months searching sequence databases, notices something weird and develops sufficient emotional attachment to the weird thing to persuade colleagues to test it. The new model is that 950 Claude agents produce thousands of weird things in a day, and the scarce scientific resource is taste: deciding which weird thing deserves a pipette.

Anthropic says the hypotheses themselves have become an object of study. It generates hundreds or thousands of candidate reports, scientists choose which ones are worth testing, and those choices are fed back into the instructions so Claude can "mimic our own scientific taste." First AI automates scientific labor. Then it automates scientists' judgment about which AI-generated labor is worth looking at. The humans remain essential for the lab work, for now, and also for saying "huh, spectacular."

Legible networks

Here is some encouraging AI safety research:

"I think I have good news about the nature of neural networks," Goodfire researcher Nick Cammarata wrote at the start of a 26,000-word blog post.

The good news is that neural networks might not be hopelessly tangled. The somewhat less good news is that Cammarata estimates it might take him 100,000 years to understand a frontier language model circuit by circuit. Still, this is optimism in AI interpretability.

The standard story is that neural networks store unrelated concepts together in individual neurons, a phenomenon called superposition. An old image model had a neuron that responded to both cats and cars, which looked like evidence that the model had crammed unrelated ideas into the same place.

Cammarata, who helped establish that interpretation, spent much of the last year looking at neurons for about 10 hours a day, seven days a week, and reconsidered. Cats and cars are not necessarily unrelated. Pictures of cat faces and cars viewed from the side often contain a V shape. The same neuron also responded to the corner of a pool table, the nose of a Japanese high-speed train and water spraying from a fire boat. It was apparently a V neuron. Images with round O shapes turned it off.

"I was looking at the same images; there was no technical change," Cammarata said. "But I now believed that neural networks defined patterns, and so I looked at it differently. It probably took me five seconds."

An interpretability problem that helped motivate years of research was solved by staring at the pictures again for five seconds. This is either a profound scientific update or an unusually expensive Where's Waldo.

Cammarata's broader claim is not that modern language models are understood. It is that their internal structures may be legible if researchers look with the right assumptions and enough effort. Critics note that the detailed evidence so far centers on Inception v1, a 2014 image model far simpler than today's frontier systems, and that researchers still understand less than 0.01% of top language models. Stanford's Chris Potts calls the post "little more than a hunch."

But Cammarata's optimistic calculation is that, if one person could understand a model in 100,000 years, large numbers of increasingly capable AI research agents might do it much faster. This produces a pleasingly circular safety plan: Build insanely smart agents, then use them to inspect the insanely smart agents before they cause trouble. Ideally one of them notices the V shape.

Anthropic Wants Power to Block Risky AI

anthropic.com  ·  zvi  ·  2026-10-20

Read →Share →
[ Analysis + ]

Technical Analysis

Anthropic proposes a regulatory regime triggered by both model scale and developer resources: models trained above 10²⁵ floating-point operations, when built by companies with more than $500 million in AI revenue or $1 billion in AI R&D spending, would face testing, public safety summaries, system cards, recurring risk reports, independent evaluation, and security obligations. The central escalation is legal authority for government to block or deter deployment when evaluations indicate a significant catastrophic risk, with civil penalties tied to global annual revenue and increasing for repeat violations.

The framework treats transparency as necessary but insufficient. Independent evaluators would review developers’ own evaluations and risk reports, while companies would need to protect model weights and training infrastructure against insiders, criminal groups, and state actors, and report suspected model-distillation attacks. Its risk categories connect familiar AI capabilities to broader failure modes: vulnerability discovery can aid both defense and attacks on critical infrastructure, biological assistance can lower barriers to weapon development, and automated AI research could accelerate the systems that create these risks. Anthropic is notably less developed on loss-of-control and automated-R&D resilience, pointing instead to detection, containment, and shutdown infrastructure as research priorities.

If adopted, this would move frontier-model governance from disclosure toward enforceable pre-deployment oversight, potentially giving regulators leverage over the largest labs while leaving ordinary AI products outside the regime. It would also create a market for qualified independent evaluators and pressure companies to treat model security like critical infrastructure security. The framework is a policy proposal rather than enacted law, so its immediate effect is agenda-setting; the difficult unresolved questions are how catastrophic risk can be measured reliably, who gets to make the blocking decision, and how to prevent emergency powers from becoming broader innovation controls.

Meta Builds a Million-GPU Training Horizon

engineering.fb.com  ·  tldr_ai  ·  2026-10-20

Meta’s Prometheus links hundreds of thousands of GPUs across multiple buildings, with a future target near one million. Its engineering challenge is continuity: keeping synchronized training alive through failures, network distance, and power swings.

Read →Share →
[ Analysis + ]

Technical Analysis

Prometheus combines hundreds of thousands of GPUs across multiple buildings into one distributed training system, with a future target of roughly one million GPUs. Its GB200 and GB300 NVL72 platforms group 72 GPUs into a tightly coupled domain, while dedicated Ethernet back-end fabrics and aggregation networks carry synchronized GPU traffic across distances up to more than 100 kilometers. Meta reports over 85% goodput for Muse pretraining, over 90% for reinforcement learning, and an improvement from nearly 1,000 interruptions per 8,000 GPUs per day to a 99% lower rate after a year of engineering work.

The difficult part is not simply installing accelerators; it is keeping a job moving when any component in a huge, synchronized machine can fail. NVL72 increases the tightly linked failure domain from eight GPUs to 72, so Meta runs 64-GPU slices with eight in-rack spares and uses parallelism-aware recovery, asynchronous delta checkpoints, predictive memory maintenance, signal-integrity testing, and automated network rerouting. The scale is striking: each NVL72 domain contains 5,184 high-speed signaling links, while the overall system uses millions of miles of fiber, tens of thousands of switches, and millions of optical modules. Power also fluctuates sharply when synchronized training phases start or stop, so Dynamo Dimmer adjusts GPU power limits gradually, supported by rack batteries, capacitors, and facility storage rather than interrupting workloads.

This infrastructure makes longer-context and larger-model training feasible while reducing the compute wasted on restarts, which directly shortens frontier-model development cycles. It also shows that reliability, cooling, networking, and utility coordination are becoming part of model capability: a faster accelerator is of limited use if the cluster cannot sustain it. Meta’s reported figures are company claims rather than an independent benchmark, but the engineering pattern is clear—future AI competition will depend not only on model design, but on operating enormous heterogeneous fleets as a single dependable computer.

Mercury 3 Pushes Diffusion Language Forward

inceptionlabs.ai  ·  tldr_ai  ·  2026-10-20

Inception Labs’ Mercury 3 is a diffusion language model claiming 1,068 tokens per second, a one-million-token context window, and major gains over its predecessor. Its proposition is straightforward: preserve diffusion’s parallelism while scaling its quality.

Read →Share →
[ Analysis + ]

Technical Analysis

Mercury 3 is a diffusion-based language model rather than the more common autoregressive design, and Inception Labs says it is over 40 times larger than Mercury 1 while reaching 1,068 tokens per second on NVIDIA GPUs. The company reports a 1M-token context window, benchmark performance comparable to or better than its named speed-optimized competitors, and a threefold improvement in intelligence from Mercury 1 mini to Mercury 3.

In an autoregressive model, text is generated strictly one token after another; diffusion language models instead begin with a more incomplete or corrupted representation and iteratively refine multiple parts of the sequence. That approach is technically challenging because the model must preserve coherent dependencies while gaining enough parallelism to beat highly optimized next-token generation. The announcement’s central result is not a new benchmark record so much as a scaling claim: Inception says the quality gains associated with larger models and training also apply to diffusion, while retaining the architecture’s speed advantage. Its own production examples include 50 completed tasks out of 50 Mind2Web navigation tasks against an unspecified frontier comparison, roughly 0.9 seconds median generation for a search application, and roughly 2.2 seconds end to end.

If the reported throughput and quality hold outside the company’s evaluations, Mercury 3 could make multi-step agents substantially more usable: every navigation, retrieval, or tool-use turn becomes cheaper and less likely to stall, leaving room for agents to try more actions within the same latency budget. That is particularly relevant to voice interfaces, tutoring, live search, and support systems where users notice pauses immediately. The evidence here is vendor-provided and the competitor names and evaluation details are not independently documented, so the broader claim that diffusion has definitively caught up remains provisional, but the release is a meaningful commercial test of whether fast diffusion inference can compete on complex tasks rather than only simple completion.

DeepSeek Opens Its Production Inference Engine

github.com  ·  hn  ·  2026-10-20

DeepInfer exposes the runtime machinery behind DeepSeek’s large-scale services, including speculative decoding, mixture-of-experts parallelism, deterministic sampling, and specialized kernels. The Apache-licensed release is powerful but hardware-specific, initially targeting NVIDIA SM100 and Huawei Ascend 950 systems.

Read →Share →
[ Analysis + ]

Technical Analysis

DeepInfer is the serving engine DeepSeek says powers its large-scale production services, now released under Apache 2.0 for offline inference of DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash. Its core mechanisms include specialized kernels, attention and mixture-of-experts parallelism, speculative decoding with confidence-scheduled verification, asynchronous CPU scheduling, batch-invariant execution, deterministic sampling, and grammar-constrained generation; the initial hardware target is unusually narrow, requiring NVIDIA SM100 GPUs or Huawei Ascend 950 NPUs, plus CUDA 13 or CANN 9.2.

The important detail is that this is more than a model wrapper: it exposes the runtime work needed to keep expensive accelerators fed while splitting prefill and decode across many devices. Speculative decoding generates draft tokens and verifies them in larger batches, while bounded replay avoids recomputing an entire decoder prefix; MoE expert parallelism and load balancing distribute routed experts across devices. Batch invariance and fixed seeds aim to preserve identical outputs despite changing batch composition, a useful property for testing and reproducible services. Still, the repository is not a turnkey cloud stack: KV-cache management, prefill/decode disaggregation, and load balancing across replicas remain outside the release.

That makes DeepInfer potentially valuable to operators studying how DeepSeek achieves serving efficiency, and to hardware teams targeting both NVIDIA and Huawei accelerator ecosystems. It could give vLLM-, SGLang-, and TensorRT-LLM-adjacent projects concrete techniques to compare or reuse, although the SM100/CUDA 13 requirements sharply limit immediate experimentation and the single supported model limits generality. The strongest near-term impact is therefore architectural transparency and a high-performance reference implementation, rather than an instantly deployable universal inference server.

Can Coding Agents Finish Real OR Work?

arxiv.org  ·  arxiv_cs_cl  ·  2026-10-20

OR-License tests agents on 31 real operations-research engagements, including business discovery, formulation, implementation, and validation. Claude Code reached 88.39% strict feasibility and a deployment-adjusted quality score of 0.87, exposing the cost of unfinished reasoning.

Read →Share →
[ Analysis + ]

Technical Analysis

OR-License evaluates LLM coding agents end to end on 31 real-world operations-research instances spanning 10 industrial scenarios, preserving the original client materials, data, deliverable formats, and information boundaries after de-identification. A controlled simulator, ORSim, answers business questions without volunteering optimization expertise, while hidden checks test feasibility and compare objective quality with solutions that real clients previously accepted. Its Deployment-Adjusted Quality metric averages expert-relative objective quality but assigns zero to infeasible deliveries; the strongest reported configuration, Claude Code, achieved 88.39% strict feasibility and 0.87 DAQ, while some models surpassed experts on objective quality among solutions that worked.

The important distinction is between producing a good-looking optimization result and completing the whole professional process. Agents must discover missing business facts, translate informal requirements into a faithful mathematical formulation, implement the solver, and validate that the output can actually be deployed. The benchmark finds that information gathering is not merely preliminary clarification: later questions often help resolve formulation or implementation problems, and task-equalized research-chain completion rates were only 24% to 45% on evaluable tasks. In other words, the difficult bottleneck is sustained closure of information gaps and error correction across a long trajectory, not just selecting an algorithm once the problem has been neatly specified.

That makes the result a useful warning against judging agentic OR systems by objective values on clean benchmark prompts. These agents may already produce competitive solutions when everything lines up, potentially lowering the cost of exploratory modeling or assisting human consultants, but an infeasible schedule, allocation, or plan can erase the value of an apparently excellent objective. The benchmark points toward systems that maintain explicit links between evidence, requirements, formulation choices, code, and validation tests; whether that can raise reliability enough for unsupervised client delivery remains open, but the failure mode is concrete and measurable rather than a vague concern about hallucination.

SciΠvot Finds Weakness in Scientific Judgment

huggingface.co  ·  hf_daily_papers  ·  2026-10-14

SciΠvot presents 248 correction-worthy decisions drawn from six months of LLM-assisted research across 26 projects. Four of five frontier models performed below chance under one central condition, suggesting that context alone does not produce strategic scientific judgment.

Read →Share →
[ Analysis + ]

Technical Analysis

SciΠvot presents a choice-based benchmark for scientific judgment: each case gives an agent's original research plan and a senior researcher's correction, then asks a model to choose using only the record available before the intervention. The 248 cases come from six months of LLM-assisted work across 26 projects in six scientific fields, making the task about recognizing when an experiment, interpretation, or next step should change rather than merely producing plausible scientific prose.

The striking result is that, at medium reasoning effort with the project record included, four of five frontier models performed below chance on the selected cases; the strongest project-condition result was 63.5%, compared with 73.4% for fresh researchers. Supplying more context actually reduced every model's accuracy at medium effort, while asking models to reconsider their answer did not consistently improve performance. That pattern matters because the difficulty is not simply missing facts: the agents must weigh noisy evidence, understand the project's evolving constraints, and detect why an apparently reasonable plan is strategically wrong. The benchmark also has an important limitation: the alternatives are drawn from cases where experts intervened, so it measures judgment on difficult, correction-worthy moments rather than all scientific decisions.

If the finding holds beyond this curated set, autonomous research systems remain substantially less reliable at deciding when to abandon or redirect a line of work than at carrying out instructions. SciΠvot gives labs a concrete way to test that weakness before allowing agents to run unattended for long periods, and it suggests that longer prompts and generic chain-of-thought-style reconsideration are not dependable substitutes for calibrated scientific oversight. The immediate downstream effect is likely better evaluation and human-in-the-loop checkpoints; claims that current agents can independently manage open-ended research should be treated cautiously until they can beat, rather than systematically second-guess, expert corrections.

A V-Shaped Clue to Neural Network Meaning

technologyreview.com  ·  mit_tr_ai  ·  2026-10-19

A new interpretation of a famous Inception neuron argues that its apparent cat-and-car confusion may reflect a shared geometric pattern. The claim is a playful but serious challenge to the assumption that neural representations are hopelessly tangled.

Read →Share →
[ Analysis + ]

Technical Analysis

Cammarata’s central example revisits a neuron in Inception v1 that responds to both cats and cars. Rather than treating this as arbitrary superposition—unrelated concepts crammed into one neuron—he argues that the neuron detects a shared V-shaped visual pattern, also found in pool-table corners, bullet-train noses, and water sprayed from a fire boat, while rounded O-like shapes suppress it. From such cases, he believes neural networks may encode coherent patterns and circuits that can be mapped with enough careful inspection and automated assistance.

Superposition is the idea that a model packs multiple features into limited neural components, which has made individual neurons look semantically chaotic and motivated efforts to “untangle” them into cleaner features. Several prominent interpretability researchers therefore view the post as a hopeful hypothesis, not a demonstrated breakthrough.

If the same regularity holds in modern language models, interpretability tools could eventually help identify why a model produces dangerous outputs, monitor internal computations, and support more targeted control or auditing. Cammarata’s claim also suggests that AI-assisted interpretability research might compress work that would otherwise take lifetimes. For now, though, the practical consequence is mainly a research direction and a morale boost for AI safety—not evidence that frontier models are close to being fully reverse-engineered; the planned results on contemporary LLMs will determine whether this is a major shift or an appealing overread of a small model.

Ropedia Unifies Human and Robot Experience

alphaxiv.org  ·  alphaxiv

Ropedia W0 trains a 14-billion-parameter video world model on human and robot footage, representing motion through camera poses and projected hand or gripper skeletons. The shared visual interface lets abundant human video inform embodied-agent simulation without pretending bodies are identical.

Read →Share →
[ Analysis + ]

Technical Analysis

Ropedia W0 is a video diffusion transformer conditioned on an initial image, text, camera motion, and projected hand or gripper skeletons. Its training combines about 10,000 hours of egocentric human video with 3,000 hours of robot data, using camera poses to represent locomotion and image-aligned skeletons to represent manipulation; the 14B model reaches 21.98 dB PSNR on a held-out eight-embodiment evaluation, versus 19.02 for Cosmos3-Nano and 18.77 for Cosmos3-Super.

The important design choice is making unlike bodies express their actions through a shared visual interface without pretending their joints are identical. Human motion supplies camera and hand annotations, while robot skeletons come from embodiment-specific forward kinematics plus manual correction where projected grippers do not line up with what the camera sees. An ablation shows that skeleton conditioning is especially important overall, dropping PSNR from 21.98 to 18.17 when removed; camera conditioning has a smaller average effect because most test embodiments barely move their viewpoints, but costs 1.3 dB on moving-viewpoint embodiments. The model therefore predicts visual consequences of motion rather than fully modeling contact or object physics.

That makes the system useful for simulating candidate actions, evaluating policies, and supplying training data or world-model rollouts for embodied agents, with distillation enabling real-time VR interaction lasting minutes. The strongest practical implication is that abundant human video can improve robot evaluations, though the transfer evidence depends on the paper's particular post-training protocol and does not establish that the model can safely plan or control a robot by itself; an external value model still chooses among imagined actions.

Technical Analysis

Analysis

Prometheus combines hundreds of thousands of GPUs across multiple buildings into one distributed training system, with a future target of roughly one million GPUs. This infrastructure makes longer-context and larger-model training feasible while reducing the compute wasted on restarts, which directly shortens frontier-model development cycles. Analysis

Ropedia W0 is a video diffusion transformer conditioned on an initial image, text, camera motion, and projected hand or gripper skeletons.
