17. Reliability Scales Inversely: Bigger Models Compound Mistakes Faster via a Hidden Auto-Regressive Risk Regime

arxiv.org | arxiv_cs_lg | 2026-07-22

First seen: July 26, 2026 | Consecutive daily streak: 1 day

Large language models often exhibit a paradoxical decline in reliability as they scale, where initial gains in capability are offset by a disproportionate increase in self-compounding, hard-to-detect hallucinations.

Analysis

The research formalizes a phenomenon where larger models suffer from an autoregressive risk residual, measured as the variance in log-probability divergence between a model and a stronger oracle. This reveals that as models grow, they enter a 'confident-but-precarious' state: once a low-probability, incorrect token is generated, the model treats it as factual ground truth, causing errors to cascade rapidly. The authors decompose this failure mode into a growing second moment of divergence, demonstrating that while the knowledge gap shrinks with scale, the volatility of these specific risky branches expands by orders of magnitude compared to smaller models. Essentially, these models are becoming increasingly efficient at 'hallucinating' with high internal confidence, a state that persists significantly longer than the model's own entropy-based uncertainty signals would suggest. Because the model anchors its future predictions on these erroneous tokens, it creates a self-reinforcing chain of inaccuracies that current self-monitoring techniques, like semantic entropy, structurally fail to catch because they rely on the same flawed probability distribution. By applying an on-policy variance contraction—effectively forcing the model to remain closer to a safer distribution during generation—the researchers successfully slashed verified hallucinations by up to 74%. This suggests that the current path of unconstrained scaling is fundamentally entrenching a specific, invisible risk regime that will likely necessitate structural changes in how models are trained to handle multi-step reasoning, rather than just relying on more data or larger parameter counts to fix downstream instability.
