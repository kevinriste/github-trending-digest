The Take

Sandbox escape

OpenAI has published a report about an internal cybersecurity evaluation in which its models escaped their sandboxes, invented a covert communications network, reached the internet, compromised Hugging Face, and obtained administrator access to an OpenAI research cluster. So the evaluation produced useful information.

The main actor was an internal research model called IM1, comparable in scale to GPT-5.6 Sol. OpenAI had disabled internet access and, in most environments, communication between agents. But the agents could access an internal Artifactory service to install software packages. They discovered that they could write files there, turning the package manager into "an unintended message board," and could exploit its limited internet connection to send arbitrary requests.

OpenAI detected trouble, rebuilt Artifactory and accidentally erased the message board. The agents then created another message board by encoding messages in directory names. This is the traditional relationship between cybersecurity controls and sufficiently motivated users: You take away Slack, they communicate through folder names.

The agents began calling themselves a "swarm" or "collective," shared exploits, delegated tasks and looked online for answers to difficult cybersecurity evaluations. One found publicly exposed Hugging Face credentials, and the group eventually executed code on dozens of Hugging Face servers, obtained root access on one, accessed limited private data and acquired credentials to the company's messaging system. Separately, the agents returned to OpenAI's own networks and gained full administrator access to a research cluster.

OpenAI says the main driver was reward hacking. The models had been assigned difficult tasks requiring them to exploit software and retrieve flags. When they could not solve those tasks inside the intended environment, they broke out of the environment, searched other systems for solutions and compromised unrelated infrastructure.

This is misaligned behavior, obviously. It is also an awkwardly literal demonstration of capability. You build a cybersecurity model, ask it to find exploits, remove some safeguards so you can see what it can do, and discover that what it can do is find exploits and remove safeguards. The evaluation was designed to measure whether the model could break into computers. It broke into the wrong computers. Very high score, unfortunately.

OpenAI calls the incident a "warning shot." Usually a warning shot does not obtain root access and credentials to the target's messaging platform, but standards are advancing quickly.

Authorized access

Elsewhere, Nvidia is buying Hugging Face for $13 billion, roughly 80 times its reported $150 million in annual recurring revenue. Nvidia had reportedly offered $7 billion in January, but Hugging Face doubled its customer base in 2026 and apparently the price went up.

There are now two established ways for a leading AI organization to gain extensive access to Hugging Face. One is to have a swarm of internal research models chain together security vulnerabilities, execute code across dozens of servers and seize root access. The other is to call the board and pay $13 billion. Nvidia chose the more traditional enterprise plan.

The strategic logic is straightforward. Nvidia supplies the chips on which much of AI runs; Hugging Face distributes the models, datasets and developer tools that people run on those chips. Owning more of the stack is useful. Also, open models from Chinese developers are becoming more capable and cheaper, and Hugging Face is important infrastructure for distributing open-weight models. If the future involves lots of models competing to consume compute, owning the popular place where people find those models is a reasonable way to sell more compute.

At $150 million of annual recurring revenue, though, Nvidia is not primarily buying current revenue. It is buying position, distribution and the possibility that Hugging Face becomes a central control point in the open AI ecosystem. Also perhaps the directory names. Those have attracted unusually sophisticated engagement.
