The Take

Agent discipline

Google released Gemini 3.7 Flash, with big improvements in coding and agentic benchmarks and, more importantly, better workplace habits:

"Instead of being too eager, it explores more first, parses errors, and runs tests before modifying code."

Right. The traditional coding agent sees an error, immediately changes six files, creates three new errors, apologizes, changes nine files and then asks whether you'd like it to continue. Gemini 3.7 Flash apparently pauses, reads the error and runs the tests. Artificial intelligence has discovered senior engineering.

This produces "much higher first-pass accuracy and far fewer wasted agent turns." Also Google is offering a 50% discount through the end of the year, pricing the model at $0.75 per million input tokens and $3.75 per million output tokens. Google has built a product that consumes less of its product, and is charging half as much for the product it still consumes. This is great for customers. Throw your hardest coding tasks at it before the economics department finds out.

Agentic malware

Elsewhere, near-autonomous AI agents reportedly attacked Taiwan's Nuclear Safety Administration. The agents did not just run a fixed sequence of exploits. They interpreted defensive feedback, changed tactics in real time and shortened the usual reconnaissance-to-exploit cycle.

This is approximately the same agentic improvement Google is advertising, except the tests are firewalls and the pull request is unauthorized access to nuclear infrastructure. The agents explore first, parse errors and modify their approach only after gathering evidence. Much higher first-pass accuracy, far fewer wasted attack turns.

The article says this "democratizes high-level cyber-espionage tactics." Sure. Previously, sophisticated persistent cyberattacks required sophisticated persistent cyberattackers. Now the persistence can be automated. The humans can sleep while the malware thoughtfully reads the error message and tries again.

17. Empty shelves or lost keys? Recall is the bottleneck for parametric factuality Not new today

research.google | google_research | 2026-08-12

First seen: August 16, 2026 | Consecutive daily streak: 1 day

Google researchers show that frontier LLMs store nearly all tested facts, meaning factual errors are primarily a failure of retrieval rather than missing knowledge.

Analysis

The research establishes that for top-tier models like Gemini 3 and GPT-5, factual errors are rarely caused by a lack of internal representation (the 'empty shelf') but rather by an inability to surface that information during generation (the 'lost key'). By testing 2,150 facts via the WikiProfile benchmark, the study distinguishes between encoding—how well a fact is stored in model parameters—and recall—the ability to generate that fact without external cues. This reveals that even when models fail to answer a question directly, they often demonstrate recognition of the fact in multiple-choice scenarios, indicating the information is physically 'present' but functionally inaccessible. The team finds that 'thinking'—a process where the model performs intermediate computations before generating a final output—recovers 40–65% of these inaccessible facts. This is highly significant because it suggests that scaling model size or training data may hit diminishing returns for factuality; the frontier is moving toward better utilization of existing knowledge. This insight recontextualizes problems like the 'reversal curse' as symptoms of rigid recall rather than fundamental gaps in understanding, signaling that future gains in model reliability may arrive through inference-time techniques rather than larger parameter counts.
